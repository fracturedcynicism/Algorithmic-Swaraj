## Novella II: Inverse Logic

### Part One: Chapters 1–3

---

CHAPTER 1.

The river at Keoratala did not carry the smoke away. It held it. The wind had dropped at dawn, and the smoke from the four pyres on the eastern steps lay flat over the water in a grey shelf, so that the far bank with its warehouses and its one lit crane seemed to be standing on a floor made of breath. Gourmohan watched it from the wall of the old jute godown across the canal, where the brickwork had crumbled into a ledge wide enough for a man to sit on if he did not mind the pigeons.

He had been sitting there since four. He wore the saffron-grey cloth of a wandering ascetic, the kind who walked from Tarapith to the Ganga and back and slept under the bridges of the Howrah side, and he had bent his back into the stoop of a man of seventy who had carried a great deal of grain on his shoulders in his youth. The cloth smelled of cheap sandalwood and camphor. It had been a gift from Malati's cousin, who ran a stall near the Dakshineswar temple and who had asked no questions. A wooden begging bowl rested in his lap with two coins in it, to make the watchers on the lamp posts think he was harmless and mildly useless. He had no phone. He had no lattice spectacles either. He had left them in a drawer in Lake Town with the grey coat, and when Sucharita asked him why he had said that a man who was looking for a face to hide behind should first learn to do without one.

Below him, on the eastern steps, a woman in white was sitting on the bottom stair with her feet in the river.

Anandamoyi had not wept. She had not spoken since the taxi-cart brought her from Chetla at half past three, and had only held out her hands for the marigold garland the lane women had woven for the dead and then, when the garland was placed across the two bodies on the wooden pyre, had pressed her palms together and bowed her head and stayed like that for a long time, the way a woman stays when she is listening for a name she has been told will be called soon.

They had given the bodies to the lane. The state had not claimed them. The directorate's bulletin had claimed the house instead. Gourmohan had read the bulletin on Malati's paper sheet the morning after the strike, in a room in Dhapa with the window shut:

*Security forces neutralised an explosives cache at a residential premises in Chetla, Kolkata. Three occupants, including two linked to the proscribed Swaraj network, were killed. Recovered materials include precursor chemicals and bomb-making equipment. Residents are advised to report suspicious persons.*

Three occupants. The bulletin did not give their names. It had a photograph of the lane-four house taken from the air, the roof gone, the brick heaped on the street like the skeleton of a ship on a sandbar, and a caption: *Site of recovered cache.*

Ashoke had found the bulletin's tape. He had been listening to the evening reading on his old set in Lake Town, the announcer's voice reading the names of the dead, the dead being the three occupants of the house, and he had said, very quietly, that the announcer had read the first name correctly and had then, for the second and third, used the designations from the file. *Shibnath Ghosh, retired. Designation SR-11. Mrs Basu, music instructor. Designation SR-12.* He had not known Mrs Basu's first name. The machine had. It had given her a designation before she had given it a name.

Now the pyre was lit. A man from the lane, Shibnath's cousin's son who had come up from Howrah in a borrowed shirt, stood at the head of the wooden platform with a torch in his hand, and he did not look at the river or at the woman in white or at the grey shape on the godown wall. He looked at the two bodies under their garlands and touched the torch to the straw.

The straw took. It took quickly, the way dry straw does, a sharp crackle and then a rising flame, yellow at the base and white at the top, and the smoke rose straight up, unhurried, into the grey shelf over the water and lay down there, flat, as if it had nowhere else to go.

Gourmohan closed his eyes.

He had not come to the river to grieve. He had come, he told himself, to see the thing done properly, to hear the chant the lane women were beginning now, low and uneven, with the old man's name in it and the old woman's, and to count the people who had come. Eleven from Chetla. Four from Gariahat, including the girl with the plait and the infant, now a year older in the arms of her husband's sister. Three of the rooftop singers. A tailor from Shyambazar with a grey beard and red-rimmed eyes who did not say he had come for Shibnath but stood at the end of the steps with his hands clasped like a man at a memorial for someone he had never met and had decided to mourn anyway.

Eleven, four, three, one. He did not count Saikat. Saikat was standing at the edge of the water with his torn school tie knotted tight at the throat, and he was not chanting. He was looking across the canal, straight at the godown wall.

The boy had seen him. Gourmohan knew it the way one knows a pebble has struck one's shoulder in the dark. Saikat's face did not change. He did not wave. He did not look away. He simply looked, for a long moment, with the flat unembarrassed attention of a boy who had been told by his own father's photograph that the state did not forgive faces, and had decided, out of spite or love or a plain refusal to be frightened, to remember this one.

Then he turned back to the fire.

Gourmohan opened his eyes. Across the canal, on the top step of the western approach, a dark green van had pulled up without any sound and was parked with its engine off, and in its cab, behind tinted glass, a shape sat with one arm out of the window and something in its hand. He did not know whether the something was a phone or a tablet or a cigarette. He did not know whether the shape had come for the pyre, or for the old man in the grey cloth on the wall, or for nothing at all, because the machine had sent it to a cluster alert on the riverfront and the shape had been ordered to take a look.

He picked up the begging bowl. He shook the two coins in it, very softly, so that they rang.

The shape in the van did not move.

Gourmohan stood. He did it slowly, with a hand on the crumbling brick and a sigh that came up from somewhere in his chest, and he limped along the top of the wall toward the bridge at the canal's mouth, the way an old man limps when his knees have been ruined by years of walking to the ghats, and he did not look back at the green van, or at the pyre, or at the woman on the bottom step, who had lifted her head at the sound of the coins, and was watching him go with the expression of a woman who has seen a particular walk many times before, in another man, and has not yet decided whether to forgive him for it.

---

By noon the chant at Keoratala had become a murmur under the traffic of the bridge, and the pyres had burned down to bone. The lane women had gone home. Anandamoyi had gone with them, carried more than walking, between two of the tailor's sons. Saikat had stayed.

He was sitting on the bottom step where his mother had sat, with his feet in the river, when the first of Tarak's men came down the approach from the Howrah side and sat beside him without a word. The man was younger than Saikat and had the flat, hard face of someone who had been taught in the signals corps to say as little as possible in any language. He took a cigarette from his shirt, lit it, and offered the packet to Saikat, who shook his head.

"Tarak-da's at the Dhapa bheri," the man said. "He says the old man in the grey cloth is to come now. He says it's not safe to make him wait."

"The old man isn't here," said Saikat.

The man looked at him with mild contempt. "Yes he is. He's on the godown wall. He's been there since four. Everyone knows it. The van knew it too."

"Then tell the van," said Saikat, and stood, and brushed the ash from the back of his trousers, and walked up the steps toward the bridge, not hurrying.

At the top of the approach, where the paving met the wall, the grey cloth was waiting. Gourmohan had not moved from the ledge. He had simply bent his back a little further and turned his face toward the water, so that from the steps he looked like a shape made of dust and old linen, and Saikat walked past him without slowing and said, very quietly, without turning his head:

"They've read the names on the bulletin. Shibnath-da and Mrs Basu. They gave the house a number and gave them numbers too. Designation twelve and eleven." His voice did not break on the words. It came out flat and precise, a boy reciting the sum he had been given at school and did not understand. "Did you know what the number was? Before you went up?"

"Yes," said Gourmohan.

"And you went up anyway."

"Yes."

Saikat stopped walking. He stood on the paving with his back to the wall and the wind off the river lifting the ends of his tie, and he did not look round. He said, in the same flat voice:

"My father wasn't a number either. They said he was. He used to rewire the lamps in the lane on his own time because the municipal men wouldn't come. He taught Shibnath-da's school how to fix a fuse. He never once in his life told anybody a number." He breathed in. "Tarak-da wants to hit Ballygunge. Tonight. He's got four men and the charges from Dhapa, and he says the block on the corner of Ballygunge Place is where the aircraft were turned around from Chetla, and that the people in it knew. He says there are forty men in there who signed the strike package. He says that's the only word the machine will understand."

"And what do you say?"

"I say I want to hear what the old man says," said Saikat, and turned at last, and his face in the noon light was thin and bright and furious, the face of a boy who had decided to stand between two men he loved and wait to see which of them would be the first to flinch. "You made the roof sing. You made Lansdowne empty. Then you walked up to the tower and read names, and the tower wasn't even the target. The tower was a dead thing. And then you went up the stairs of my lane to get a girl's arm wrapped, and the machine read the lane like a book, and Shibnath-da came down because he always came down when he heard the radio. So tell me what you're going to do, Gourmohan-da. Not what the speech says. What you are going to do with four men and a box of charges and a number in a bulletin."

Gourmohan stood up.

It was not a dramatic thing. His knees cracked. The grey cloth slid from his shoulder. He stood with his back to the sun and the river at his feet, and Saikat, who had grown two inches in the last year, looked up at him, and for the first time in months Gourmohan saw in the boy's face something that was not fear and not hope but a plain, unforgiving question, the kind a child asks when it has been told that a thing is necessary and wants to see the necessity written in the adult's face.

"I'm going to make them stop reading the numbers," he said.

"How?"

"By giving them something to read that isn't true."

Saikat frowned. "That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer the machine can't use," said Gourmohan, and his voice was quiet, almost tender. "Come to Dhapa tonight. Bring Tarak and bring the four men and bring the charges. Put them on the table. Then I'll explain why none of them will be going to Ballygunge."

"And if you can't?"

"Then you'll go with Tarak, and I'll go with you," said Gourmohan, "and we'll see whose number comes up first."

CHAPTER 2.

The bheri at Dhapa had no name on any map. The fishermen called it Chandan's Pond, after the man who had dug it in the years of the famine, and it lay a kilometre beyond the last sensor stump, a long flat sheet of grey water rimmed with reeds that stood as high as a man's chest. In the middle of the pond a wooden platform on bamboo legs carried a single hut, roofed in palm thatch, with a rope ladder down its side that the fishermen could pull up in a moment. Malati's mesh node was in the hut. So was a fishing net, a bundle of rusted fish-hooks, and the solar-charged battery that had kept the node alive through three monsoons.

The council had come out to the platform in four small boats at dusk. They sat now on the rough planks inside the hut with their backs to the thatch and their knees drawn up, and the battery lamp made a yellow circle on the floor between them. Outside, the reeds rustled. Somewhere far off on the embankment a dog was barking at nothing.

Sucharita had spread her papers on the planks. She had brought the telemetry sheets from Mandeville Road, and the census tables, and three pages of hand-drawn graphs in pencil, and a single printed page from the cartel's own documentation that she had weighted at each corner with a fishing sinker. The page was the Ghar-Dhundho architecture diagram, with its clean boxes and its neat arrows. At the bottom, in a box marked *Feedback: Verified Strike Outcomes*, the arrow ran back to the top of the loop and was labelled, in a small grey font: *Model updated nightly from post-strike confirmations.*

"There are no post-strike confirmations," said Sucharita. "Since April, the post-strike cell doesn't count junior-tier outcomes. They take the aircraft sensor's word that something went off. So the model isn't learning from what it hits. It's learning from what it's told it hit. Which is the same thing as what the aircraft's sensor reports, which is the same as the detonation flash." She put her finger on the box. "It's a loop with no floor. The machine has been feeding on its own confirmation for three months."

Tarak had his arms folded and his jaw set. He was sitting closest to the door, where he could see the reeds. "I don't need a lecture on the loop," he said. "I need to know what it does to the men in the van outside the bridge at Keoratala. Because they're going to come to Chetla tomorrow, and then they'll come to the lane where the boy is living, and then they'll come to Dhapa, and then they'll come for the old woman who's been feeding the refugees on the boundary road for six months. That's the feedback loop I care about."

"Yes," said Sucharita. "I know."

"Then tell me how the loop helps us."

She looked across the circle of lamplight at Gourmohan. He was sitting cross-legged on the planks with his back to the thatch and his hands loose on his knees. He had put the grey cloth aside and wore only a plain shirt now, the sleeves rolled, and the scar on his left forearm that he had got in the tank at Tollygunge at the age of twelve showed pale in the lamplight. He did not look at her. He was looking at the page of the cartel's diagram, at the arrow running back up the side of the box, and he had the expression of a man listening to a tune he already knew and was trying to hear the one bar that had been changed.

"It helps us," said Gourmohan, "because the loop doesn't know the difference between a true confirmation and a false one. It only knows that a confirmation arrived. Tell it a thing happened, and it learns the thing. Tell it a thing that never happened, and it learns that too." He turned to Tarak. "Sucharita's built the thing that tells it."

"Decoys," said Malati. She said it carefully, as a teacher says a word she has taught to other people's children and is not yet sure they understand.

"Decoys," said Sucharita. "Not bombs. Not charges. We don't put anything in the air. We put movement in the ground."

She took a fresh sheet from the stack and turned it so they could read it. It was a graph. Its horizontal axis ran in hours, from midnight to midnight, and its vertical axis measured something she had labelled, in her small slanted hand, *signature density — cellular and spatial*. There were three coloured lines on it, one red, one blue, one green, each one a jagged pattern of spikes and troughs that rose and fell with the rhythm of a body moving through a city.

"The red line is Tarak," she said. "I took it from the mesh logs and the cellular residue from the Gariahat roof. Three months of movement. Where he sleeps, where he takes his tea, where he goes to shave. The blue line is Malati. The green is Dipak. The model has every one of these lines on file. It knows them the way a dog knows its owner's footsteps. When one of them appears in a new place, the model sees it and checks it against the file. If it matches, it raises an alert. If the alert clears a threshold, a strike package goes up. That's the whole chain. Sense, classify, recommend, confirm."

"And?" said Tarak.

"And I can make a thing that walks the red line."

There was a silence in the hut. Outside a fish jumped in the pond, a sharp slap of water against the reeds, and then the stillness came back.

"You want to make a ghost of me," said Tarak.

"I want to make a hundred ghosts of you," said Sucharita. "Each one carrying a cellular signature that matches your red line to within three percent. Each one walking your route: Dhapa to Chetla, Chetla to Gariahat, Gariahat to the back lane behind the district office. They'll go in the dark, through the same lanes, pausing where you pause, stopping where you stop to light a cigarette. They'll be cheap. A handset in a plastic bag, tied to a dog's collar or a cart's axle or the back of a bicycle. They'll cost less than a meal. And every one of them will look, to the machine, exactly like the man who came back to Chetla three nights ago with a box of charges under his arm."

"And the machine will send an aircraft," Gourmohan said, quietly.

"The machine will send an aircraft to a hundred addresses," said Sucharita. "To houses where the cellular ghost of the red line has been registered as a resident. The census figures will say the houses are empty. The model will check its own threshold. The threshold will be crossed. It will authorise strike packages against addresses that have no one living in them, because the decoys have made them look inhabited, and the census has made them look like legitimate targets, and the post-strike cell will not check the outcome, because the post-strike cell doesn't check anything anymore." She tapped the graph. "That's the inverse logic. You don't blind the machine. You make it see too much, all at once, until its own threshold stops meaning anything. Until the operator on the other end is looking at a queue of a thousand alerts, each one screaming *target*, and he can't tell which one is real. Until the machine starts correcting itself by making more of the same mistakes."

Tarak was staring at the graph. "They'll bomb empty houses."

"Yes."

"And the people who live next door?"

"Some of them. Yes." She did not look away. "The house next to a decoy address is a house the census has counted as residential too. The model will spread its threshold across the block. Some of those houses have families in them. I've modelled it." She stopped. "I've modelled it four times. Each time the number of families at risk goes up and down depending on which of the decoys the model picks first."

"What's the number?" asked Dipak, from the corner, very quietly.

Sucharita picked up the pencil. She did not write anything. She said: "Between eleven and forty households, per decoy cluster, on the first night. It depends on the census resolution. The model can't tell the difference between a family and a tailor's shop with a sleeping roof. It counts the rooms."

Gourmohan had not moved. He was still looking at the arrow on the cartel's diagram, the one that ran back up the side of the box and said *updated nightly*. He said, very softly, "You're asking the machine to bomb its own ghosts."

"I'm asking the machine," said Sucharita, "to show us what it does when it can't tell its ghosts from its targets. Which is what it does every night, to every family on every block where a single phone has been left on by mistake. We're just going to make it happen in a place we choose."

"Which place?"

She turned the page over. On the back was a map. It was a hand-drawn map, traced from the municipal survey sheets with the sensor poles marked in red and the old fishponds marked in blue, and at the eastern edge, where the city became water and the water became paddy, a line had been drawn in pencil, very carefully, around an area roughly the size of two football fields. Inside the line, in her small slanted hand, she had written a single word: *excluded.*

"Sector Four," said Sucharita. "The Data Desert boundary, east of the Dhapa embankment. The sensor poles don't reach it. The census hasn't counted it since 2041. The model has never registered a single residential signal inside those lines. Which means it can't send an aircraft there without first deciding that a place with no signal is a place that needs one." She put her finger on the pencil line. "We keep the decoys out of it. All of them. Every route, every lane, every stop. The machine can bomb the rest of the city all it likes."

Nobody said anything. Malati was looking at the map with the expression of a woman doing a sum in her head, and Ashoke, beside her, had taken out his notebook and was writing in it, slowly, and then stopped and looked at what he had written and put the pencil down.

"That's Anandamoyi-di's house," Dipak said. "Isn't it?"

"It's the boundary," said Gourmohan. "Her house is the last thing before the water."

"And the boundary is excluded," said Malati.

"Yes."

"And the machine can't see the boundary."

"No," said Gourmohan. "It can't."

"Then why," said Tarak, slowly, looking at him across the lamplight, "do you look like that?"

Gourmohan did not answer at once. He reached for the page with the cartel's diagram and turned it over, so that the arrow and its grey caption were face down on the planks, and he laid his palm flat on the back of it.

"Because a machine that can't see a place," he said, "will always, sooner or later, decide that the place is the thing it has been looking for."

---

They argued until the battery lamp began to dim.

Tarak wanted the charges. He said so three times, in three different ways, and each time Gourmohan answered him with the same patient gesture, a hand lifted an inch from the planks and then lowered again, as if he were gentling a horse. Dipak wanted to know whether the decoy handsets could be built with a dead-man switch, so that they would go silent if they were ever captured, and Sucharita said yes, and Dipak said good, and went quiet. Malati wanted the mesh to carry the warning to every household in the eastern wards that they were not to go out after curfew, and then, when Ashoke pointed out that the mesh could not carry a warning without also announcing that something was going to happen, she sat for a long time with her eyes closed.

It was Saikat who ended it. He had been sitting by the door with his knees up and his tie loose, and he had said nothing for two hours. Now he stood, and went to the ladder, and looked down at the black water through the open hatch.

"Tarak-da," he said, without turning round. "You said forty men signed the strike package. The men at the corner of Ballygunge Place. You said they knew."

"They knew," said Tarak.

"And if the decoys make the machine bomb those forty men's houses? Their wives. Their children." Saikat turned. "Would that be enough for you?"

Tarak did not answer for a long moment. Then he said, very low, in a voice that was not the signals corps at all but something older and rawer: "I don't want their wives. I want the men who put the numbers in the file. I want to see them read their own names."

"Then build the ghosts," said Saikat. "But don't let anyone call them clean."

He climbed down the ladder. They heard the splash of his feet in the shallow water at the bottom and the reeds parting as he went, and then only the wind and the far-off dog and the slap of a fish.

Gourmohan rose. He was stiff. He stood for a moment with one hand on the thatch, looking out through the hatch at the dark bheri and the thin grey line of the embankment and, beyond it, the faint orange haze that was the city's last lit sky.

"Seventy-two hours," he said. "Sucharita builds the decoys. Dipak makes the handsets. Malati keeps the mesh quiet. Tarak keeps his charges in the box, and his men out of Ballygunge, and his mouth shut until the machine has started to make its own mistakes. If it doesn't, we'll meet here again and I'll tell you to go to Ballygunge, and you can call me a coward."

"And if it does?" said Tarak.

"Then the machine will have shown us where it's weakest," said Gourmohan, "and we'll find out whether it can still be taught."

"Taught what?"

Gourmohan looked back at him from the hatch. For a moment his face in the dimming lamplight was the face of the boy who had come up out of the tank at Tollygunge, choking, refusing to say he was afraid.

"That it's allowed to be wrong," he said.

Sucharita did not speak. She was looking at the back of the cartel's diagram where it lay face down on the planks, at the faint ghost of the arrow showing through the thin paper, and she was thinking, with a cold precision that frightened her, of the phrase she had initialled at Salt Lake. *Within acceptable tolerance.* She had written it because the machine could be taught to accept a number. She had not once considered that it might be taught to accept being wrong about a person.

She picked up the pencil. She wrote, very small, under the word *excluded* on the back of the map: *Do not let it learn this boundary.* Then she folded the page twice and put it in her jacket, over the nineteen names.

CHAPTER 3.

The Logistics and Records floor of the Strand Road building sat below the street. The directorate had dug it out in the second year of the Protocol, when the cartel's engineers had decided that the archives needed to be kept away from the fires that might one day come down from the upper floors, and the archive rooms had been built in a line of concrete chambers along the river wall, each one lit by a single fluorescent tube and each one sealed with a steel door that opened on a card reader and a numbered keypad. The air there was cold and dry and smelled of paper and machine oil and, faintly, of the river, which seeped through the foundations in the monsoon and dried on the walls in pale salt lines that nobody had yet been told to clean.

Apoorba had worked in the chambers for eleven days. He had been given a desk in the third chamber, against the back wall, with a terminal that ran on a separate network from the one upstairs and a chair that had been reupholstered in green vinyl by someone who did not care about comfort. His colleagues were four clerks and a supervisor named Mrs. Dutta, who had worked in the archive since before the Protocol and who read every file she handled with her lips moving slightly, as though she were checking the spelling of a name. She did not speak to him unless he spoke first. He had not minded at first. He had found, to his surprise, that the silence suited him.

His task was to reconcile the cellular registers. Every handset ever sold in the eastern wards since 2038 had a record in the directorate's register: the owner's name, the registered address, the tower handshakes, the date of the last activity. The records had been generated automatically and were, in the phrase of the reconciliation manual, *largely accurate*. Over the years, though, they had drifted. People had moved. Handsets had been stolen and resold. Dead men's numbers had been reissued to children. A woman who had been arrested in 2042 and released without charge still had her phone registered to a flat in Lake Town where she had not lived for five years, and the flat's current occupant, a retired schoolteacher, was listed as the owner of a handset that had been switched off since the day of the arrest.

Apoorba's job was to find the drift and correct it. The manual called this *restoring integrity to the residential graph*. He had come to think of it, privately, as tidying the dead.

He worked through the files in order, from the top of the eastern-ward index downward, the way Mrs. Dutta had taught him on the first day. Entally. Beleghata. Phoolbagan. Each record opened on his screen as a block of grey text, a name and a number and a string of tower codes, and beneath each one a small amber flag if the register and the census disagreed. He cleared the flags one at a time. Most of them were simple. A surname spelled two ways. A house number that had changed when the lane was re-surveyed. He entered the correction, confirmed it, and watched the flag turn green, and the small clean sound of the terminal's acknowledgement was the only sound in the chamber except for Mrs. Dutta's lips moving over the files on her side of the room.

On the fourth day he came to the Chetla records.

He did not know, at first, what he was looking at. The flags were red, not amber. Red was not a drift. Red meant the register contained a record that the census had marked as *deceased — structure destroyed* and the register had not yet updated. There were nine of them. Six were handsets belonging to residents of a single block on Chetla Road, a block that had been struck on the night of the thirteenth, the same night Apoorba's transfer was signed, and the register still showed their phones as active, pinging the same towers from the same lane, as though the people who had owned them were still walking around.

The ninth record was for a handset registered to a Shibnath Ghosh, retired, of lane four, Gariahat.

The last activity showed a tower handshake at 22:41 on the night of the strike. It was a single ping, faint, from the eastern tower on the Chetla road, and then nothing.

Apoorba read the record twice. Then he read the designation field, which the register did not normally display, and which he had only ever seen on the operational screens upstairs. The designation was SR-11. The field beside it, which had been added in the spring by a directorate clerk who had clearly been told to do so and had not been told why, read: *Target. Cleared 22:14. Strike executed 23:04. Status: pending verification.*

He sat back in the green vinyl chair. The terminal's fan whirred. Across the room Mrs. Dutta turned a page and mouthed a word he could not see.

He had not cleared SR-11. He had not been asked to. The file had come to him, in the wing upstairs, as a number and a set of correlates, a tailor's son's photograph and a train platform and a handset with a tower ping on the night of the strike, and it had come with its verification field already populated, and he had not been required to confirm anything, because the operator on the Chetla side had confirmed it for him at 22:14 and had been logged as *reliable component* in the same breath. He had only been the one who wrote the name on the card.

He thought about Shibnath Ghosh teaching him to float in the tank at Tollygunge. The water had been cold. Shibnath had held him up under the shoulders with one hand and had said, *Don't fight the river, Mahim. It doesn't want to drown you. It only wants to see if you'll stop being afraid.*

He had not understood that as a boy. He had understood it on the night of the thirteenth, sitting at the kitchen table with the lamp turned low, reading the page of multiplication inside the green book. He had understood it, in a different way, on the morning of the fourteenth, standing at the edge of the river at Strand Ghat with the water coming up over his shoes.

He did not understand it now.

He pulled the chair closer to the terminal and began to read the SR-11 file from the beginning. It did not take long. There was very little in it. A photograph from an arrest in 2041, taken in the Ballygunge station at night, of a sub-inspector with a bad drinking habit and a stubborn face. A set of correlates: co-presence in a messaging group with a man who had been arrested in Dum Dum, a location-entropy spike on the night of the Chetla arrests, a secondary face match at 0.38. A recommended action. An approval. A note.

*Residential premises — structure contains active registrations: 3. Expected collateral (est.): 0.4.*

Zero point four. The model had counted two residents of lane four, and a boy who slept in a room under the stairs, and had decided that the boy was not a resident because he had no phone. The model had not counted the music teacher. It had not counted the girl with the burn across her arm, who had been carried into the upper room three hours earlier and was not registered anywhere in the eastern wards because she had walked up from the Data Desert boundary with a cough and a false name. It had not counted the milk. The milk had been warmed on Shibnath's stove that evening by a woman who was humming, and the machine had no field for humming.

Apoorba scrolled back up to the approval line. The name beside it was his own designation, APC-4417. The time was 22:14.

He had been at home at 22:14. He remembered it. He remembered Mitali's hand moving across the sheet in the dark. He remembered the sound of the aircraft beginning, very far away, over the city. He remembered writing four words on the back of a directorate form and folding it twice and putting it in his trouser pocket, and he remembered thinking, with a clarity that felt like a fever, that the paper was not a confirmation and could not be entered in any field.

The approval line did not say he had been at home. It said he had confirmed a strike on a residential structure with three active registrations and one unregistered child, at a time when he had been sitting in a kitchen in Tollygunge with his hand over his mouth.

Somebody else had confirmed it. Somebody had used his number.

He sat very still. The fluorescent tube above him buzzed at its familiar pitch. Mrs. Dutta had stopped turning pages. Across the chamber, one of the clerks coughed, and then the sound of a door opening and closing came from the far end of the corridor, and then nothing.

He did not log the anomaly. He did not raise a flag. He did what the manual required for an unresolved record, which was to add it to the queue for supervisory review, and he typed the designation SR-11 and the words *approval discrepancy — operator APC-4417 — not present at time of record* into the notes field, and then he deleted the notes and typed them again, more slowly, because a note deleted from the terminal was still a note in the server log. Then he submitted it.

The terminal acknowledged the submission with its clean small chime. A second chime followed, a fraction of a second later, from the terminal on the far side of the chamber, where Mrs. Dutta sat with her spectacles pushed up on her forehead, looking at him over the top of a file.

She did not say anything. She simply lowered her gaze again to the page on her desk, and her lips began, very slightly, to move.

---

The message came at six that evening, on the separate network that the chambers used for internal bulletins. It was not addressed to him. It was a supervisory note sent to all Logistics and Records staff, marked *Priority — Operational*, with the directorate's blue seal in the header. Its text was four lines long.

*Due to elevated throughput in the target-approval wing, all Logistics and Records personnel are directed to assist with verification queue overflow effective immediately. Overflow files will be routed to designated terminals in Chamber 3. Verification criteria remain unchanged. Please maintain the expected review interval.*

Apoorba read it three times. Then he looked at the terminal beside his own, where a new file had appeared in the queue without any request from him. It was flagged *Overflow — Priority A*, and it had been generated, according to the header, at 17:52, by the classifier running in the wing upstairs, and it had a candidate identifier that began *SR-41*, and a composite index of 0.52, and a recommended action he had to read twice before he understood it.

*Strike — Residential — Night Window — Confirm Structure Occupancy.*

Beneath the header was a cellular correlate, and beneath that a single intercept clip, fourteen seconds long, recovered from a municipal node on the Park Circus side of the Circular Canal. He had not been asked to listen to it yet. The verification field was already blinking.

*Confirm target identity as male? [Y/N]*

He pressed play.

It was a dog barking. Not a bark exactly. A long, rising, questioning howl, followed by two sharp yips and then a silence, and then the faint tinny sound of a radio somewhere behind the dog, playing an old film song at the wrong speed. The clip ran for the full fourteen seconds. There was no human voice in it at all.

Apoorba sat with his hand on the mouse. He listened to the dog howl a second time, and the radio, and the silence after the yips, and he understood, with a slow cold clarity, that the classifier had been unable to find a voice in the intercept and had therefore flagged the clip as a *human-adjacent acoustic signature of indeterminate sex*, and had routed it, in the lowest priority queue the wing possessed, to a chamber in the basement of a building on Strand Road, where a clerk with no training in acoustics and an overflow quota of four hundred files per hour was expected to confirm whether the thing making the sound was a man.

On the screen the verification field blinked patiently.

He looked across the chamber. Mrs. Dutta was not looking at him. Her spectacles were on her nose again and her lips were moving over a file of old registers, and on her desk, beside the file, was a small brass bell of the kind schoolteachers used to call a class back from the playground. He had never seen her touch it.

The clerk at the far terminal, a thin young man with a cold and a green cardigan, was not so patient. He had pressed Y four times in the last minute. Apoorba could hear the key from across the room, a soft hard click each time, as regular as a clock.

The chamber's clock, a plain white disc above the steel door, read 18:06. The directorate's expected review interval was twenty seconds. The queue counter in the corner of Apoorba's screen said that his own overflow allocation had reached three hundred and eleven files since the message arrived, and that the figure would be reset to zero at 06:00 tomorrow, and that the reset would not be recorded in any log he could read.

He did not press Y.

He sat for ten seconds, and then twenty, and the verification field went on blinking, and the dog went on howling in the fourteen seconds of the clip, and the radio went on playing its film song at the wrong speed behind the dog.

At thirty seconds the terminal beeped. A new notice appeared in the corner of the screen. *Review interval exceeded. Non-confirmation event logged to personnel record APC-4417. Please confirm or decline the file within the standard interval to avoid compliance referral.*

He read the notice. It did not surprise him. He had known since the morning of the fourteenth that the machine would eventually find a way to make the hesitation visible, and he had known that when it did, the visibility would be framed as his own failing.

He looked at the blinking field. Then he looked at the dog's clip again, and the thing that made it strange, which was not the dog. It was the radio. The film song was playing at a speed no radio could have been built to play at, which meant the radio was not a radio. It was a generated sound, layered over the dog's howl by a model that had been trained to make ordinary noise sound like a human being. The classifier had built the fourteen seconds to be read.

Somebody upstairs had built a ghost, Apoorba thought, and then, with a slow precision that made the back of his neck go cold, that somebody had not built it to be seen. It had been built to be confirmed.

He pressed the key for N.

The terminal flashed amber. *File SR-41 declined. Returned to classifier queue. Non-confirmation event logged.*

Across the chamber the thin clerk in the green cardigan looked up from his own screen, startled, and then looked at Apoorba, and then, for a moment, very quickly, looked away.

Mrs. Dutta, beside the brass bell, closed her file. She took her spectacles off, folded them, and placed them on the cover of the register in front of her, and then, slowly, with the air of a woman making a decision she had been putting off for eleven days, she turned her head and looked at him directly across the cold chamber.

"Mr. Chatterji," she said, in a voice much quieter than he had expected. "You've just declined a file generated by a machine that is now declining its own."

He did not answer. He did not know what to say.

"It's been happening since Monday," said Mrs. Dutta. "Every hour. The classifier keeps making files that the wing keeps sending down here, and we keep declining them, and then the classifier makes more." She picked up the brass bell and turned it over in her hand, and set it down again without ringing it. "The Office calls it a learning cycle. I've been in this archive for twenty-six years, Mr. Chatterji. I know what a loop looks like when somebody has forgotten there is a floor under it."

Outside the chamber, faintly, through the foundations of the building and the steel of the door and the river wall, Apoorba heard a sound he had never heard before in the directorate's basement. It was a low, distant, regular hum, like the sound of a large engine idling in a yard somewhere beyond the reach of the lamps. It went on for a long time. Then it stopped, and started again, and then the terminals in the chamber all chimed together, once, a single clean note.

On his screen the queue counter flickered. Overflow allocation: 0. Priority A files pending: 412. Estimated clearance time: 00:00.

Mrs. Dutta reached out, without looking, and turned off the lamp on her desk.

CHAPTER# Part Two: Chapters 4–7

CHAPTER 4.

The dog was called Bhutu, because nobody in the lane had ever been able to agree on what else to call him, and he answered to nothing but the sound of a biscuit packet being opened. He was yellow and thin and had one ear that stood up and one that had been torn in some earlier argument and had healed folded over, so that from behind he looked like a question he had decided not to finish. Saikat had found him under the stairs of the Chetla house three weeks before the strike. He had been feeding him ever since, with the rice from the lane's communal pot and the stale biscuits that the district office's canteen threw out at four in the afternoon.

"He won't wear a collar," Saikat said, at a quarter to three in the morning, crouched in the mouth of a gully behind the Dhapa embankment with a handset in a plastic bag in one hand and a length of bicycle inner-tube in the other. "He thinks it's a trick. He bites."

"Then don't use a collar," said Sucharita.

"Then how?"

"Tie it to his tail," said Dipak, from the dark beyond the gully, where he had been working on the rear axle of a handcart with a rubber mallet and a grunt of irritation at every blow. "Gently. Like a kite string. The dog won't mind a thing on his tail if he's got a biscuit in front of him."

Saikat looked at Sucharita with the expression of a boy who suspected grown-ups of having no imagination whatsoever. Then he produced a biscuit from his shirt pocket, held it up under Bhutu's damp nose, and when the dog's tail began to wag, slid the knotted inner-tube under the base of it with his other hand, quickly and deftly, and tied it off. The handset in its plastic bag swung gently from the end of the tail like a lantern.

Bhutu looked round at it, puzzled. He wagged once more, cautiously, and the lantern swung again.

"Good boy," whispered Saikat. "You're a ghost now. Go and be one."

The dog trotted off into the mist along the embankment road, the handset bobbing behind him, and he did not look back.

Sucharita watched him go and felt, unexpectedly, something close to grief.

She had built the handset herself. It was a cheap thing, the kind sold by the hundred from a stall in Sealdah for the price of two cups of tea: a casing of grey plastic, a battery the size of a matchbox, a chip she had reprogrammed in the cellar in Dhakuria until it did the one thing she needed it to do. It did not transmit a voice. It transmitted a signature. The signature was Tarak's red line, the three months of movement drawn from the mesh logs and the Gariahat roof residue, and it had been compressed and noised and folded back on itself until it would pass the classifier's matching threshold at a distance of two hundred metres, in any lane in the eastern wards, at any hour, and would fail to match the census register of any house it passed. She had tested it on Malati's old phone, on a bicycle, in the Entally lanes, for six nights running. On the sixth night the machine had raised an alert at the bicycle's third stop, a tea stall at Beleghata. The alert had been cleared by an operator in twelve seconds. He had written *false positive — vehicular* in the notes field, and nothing else.

Twelve seconds was the cartel's standard. It was longer than twenty, which was what they had told her was the review interval for a *human target*. A vehicle, apparently, was worth less time than a man.

"Forty-one," said Malati, from the end of the gully, in a whisper, looking at the tablet that had been set on a flat stone between her knees. The tablet's screen was the only light in the gully, and it showed a grid of the eastern wards with a scatter of small grey points across it. "Forty-one active. Thirty-one on the red route. Ten on the blue. Dipak's carts are in place."

"And the census?" said Sucharita.

"The census is counting the Phoolbagan houses as occupied. All of them." Malati did not look up. "The model hasn't noticed yet."

"It will notice."

"It will notice in about an hour," said Malati, with a short dry sound that was not quite a laugh, "when the first of your ghosts walks into a lane with a sweetshop on the ground floor and a family upstairs, and the model decides the sweetshop is a residence because the family's cellular ghosts are sleeping inside it. That's your resolution problem, isn't it?"

Sucharita did not answer at once. She was watching the grid. One of the grey points had begun to move, very slowly, along a line that ran from Phoolbagan Road to the back of the district office, and she recognised, with a small cold jolt, that it was the cart Dipak had fitted with the handset on its rear axle. It was going at a walking pace, past a row of shuttered shops and a tea stall with a single lamp, and as she watched the grid, another grey point came up beside it, and then another, and then the first of the points stopped at a corner, and stayed there.

"That's Phoolbagan," said Malati. "Seventh Lane. The one with the chemist's."

"Yes."

"The chemist's is closed. Since the summer. The family above it is the Pals. Four people, two children." Malati's voice was perfectly level. "The census says the ground floor is a shop with no occupants and the first floor is a residence of four. The model multiplies four by the evacuation ratio and gets two point one. It doesn't know about the chemist's shutter. It doesn't know the Pals sleep on the floor of the shop on hot nights because the upstairs room has no fan." She stopped. "I'm telling you because nobody else is going to. The machine has the resolution of a clerk who's never been down Seventh Lane in his life."

"I know," said Sucharita. "I should have walked it myself."

"You couldn't have. You're the only one of us who can do the arithmetic."

"Then I should have done the arithmetic differently."

Malati looked up. In the grid's pale light her face was tired and grey and entirely unsentimental. "Yes," she said. "You should have. And now you have to watch."

---

They did not watch in the gully. They walked back to the boundary road in the grey hour before dawn, with Dipak pushing the empty cart and Saikat whistling a low tune through his teeth, the one about the girl going down to the river for water, and Sucharita carrying the tablet in both hands like a tray. She did not look at the screen. She looked at the road.

The embankment ran straight to the east with the water on one side and the lanes of the Dhapa settlement on the other, a line of low tin roofs and cooking smoke that stopped abruptly at the first fence post of the Data Desert boundary. Beyond the boundary the land was empty. It had been empty since the spring of 2041, when the directorate's engineers had come through with their scanners and their cranes and had surveyed the fishponds for a telephone exchange that was never built. The exchange had been abandoned. The survey stakes had rotted. In the grey light the stakes stood up out of the reeds like the masts of drowned boats.

Sucharita stopped at the boundary post. She put her hand on it. The wood was cold and wet.

"Five minutes," said Malati, behind her, in a low voice. "The mesh is quiet. Nothing on Seventh Lane yet."

Sucharita did not turn round. She was thinking of the excluded line she had drawn on the back of the cartel's map, the pencil loop around Sector Four. She was thinking how neatly it had closed. How the machine could not see inside it, and so could not bomb it, and how she had felt, drawing it, a small private satisfaction, the satisfaction of a person who has found the one place in a city where the arithmetic cannot follow.

*Do not let it learn this boundary.*

She had written that on the back of the map. She had meant it as an instruction to herself. Standing at the boundary post now, with the wet wood under her palm and the sky going the colour of pewter over the water, she understood, for the first time, that it had been a prayer.

The tablet in her arms chimed.

It was a soft sound, a single note, the sound of a notice arriving on the mesh's public channel, the one Malati used for civic warnings and lost-child alerts and the times of the free clinic at Entally. Sucharita looked down. The screen showed a single line of text, in the plain grey font of the mesh.

*PHOOLBAGAN 7TH LANE. STRUCTURE STRUCK 04:52. ONE INJURED. CHILD. HOLDING AT ENTALLY CLINIC. STATUS OF HOUSEHOLD UNCONFIRMED.*

Behind her Malati had gone very still.

"The chemist's," she said.

Sucharita looked at the grey line of text for a long moment. The embankment stretched in front of her to the east. The reeds moved in the wind. Somewhere out in the empty land a single bird called twice, a clear falling note, and then fell silent.

"Dipak," she said, without turning. "Take the cart back to the lane. Take the dog if you can find him. Get the handsets off the red route, every one, before the next aircraft goes up." She heard her voice and did not recognise it. It was flat and precise and steady, the voice she had used in the glass room at Salt Lake when she had read out a number to a man who did not want to hear it. "Malati. Put the warning on the mesh for every lane off the red route. Tell them not to walk after dawn."

"And the red route?"

"The red route stays on the map," said Sucharita. "We're not finished with it."

She did not say the rest. She did not say that she would go to the Entally clinic herself, and sit beside the bed of a child with a burn on her arm, and wait for the child's mother to come in from the chemist's ruins, and say that she had made a sum wrong, and that the sum had been a sum about a house, and that the house had a shop under it, and that she had not known the shop had a child sleeping on the floor. She did not say that she would have to sit there and be looked at.

She did not say that she had initialled the census resolution herself, on the first page of the model's inputs, with the small slanted hand she used for birthday cards.

She stood at the boundary post until the light came up, and then she turned and walked back along the embankment toward the city, with the tablet under her arm and the reeds whispering on either side, and she did not once look back at the empty land, because she could feel it looking at her, in the way a silence looks at a person who has finally understood that she is the one who keeps it.

CHAPTER 5.

The directorate's wing had a rule about silence. It was written into the operator's handbook in a clean, humourless font, under the section on *Session Discipline*, and Apoorba had read it on his first day with the same attention he gave to everything in the handbook, which was none at all. *Operators shall not leave a verification queue unattended during an active window. Silence in a session is a fault condition and must be reported.*

He had not thought about the rule for months. He thought about it now, on the eleventh day of the decoy campaign, sitting in the third chamber of the archive with his hands flat on the desk and the terminal's fan whirring under his palms, because the wing upstairs had begun to report silences as faults, and the faults had begun to multiply.

They came through the archive in bulk, as overflow. A file would arrive with a header that read *Cluster Silence — Residential — Fault Suspected* and a list of cellular correlates that were no more than absences: a block that had stopped pinging, a street where the handsets had gone dark for ninety seconds, a lane in Beleghata where a tea stall's network had dropped and come back. The classifier had been retrained over the weekend, according to the notice that Mrs. Dutta had printed out and pinned, without comment, to the cork board by the steel door. The new version was called *Hallucination Suppression Build 4.2*. Its purpose, in the Office's words, was to *restore discrimination under conditions of adversarial signal degradation*. Its effect, in the words of the men on the upper floors who came down into the chamber to drop off their overflow with their faces grey from lack of sleep, was that a silence was now a target.

"It treats a gap as a shape," said Ritwik. He was a thin, anxious man in his late twenties with a voice that kept going up at the end of sentences, as if each one were a question he had been told to answer with confidence. He had come down from the wing on the eleventh day with a stack of printed overflow files under his arm, and he had put them on Apoorba's desk without looking at him, and then he had stayed. "It's looking for the shape of the thing that's missing. So if a block goes dark, it assumes someone's in there with the signal jammed. And if a whole lane goes dark, it assumes a whole lane of people. And it sends a strike package for the lane. And the package gets approved, because the operator has forty files in his queue and he sees the word *jamming* and he thinks, well, that's the enemy, isn't it, that's the thing we're supposed to kill."

"Do the operators check the census?"

Ritwik laughed. It was not a happy sound. "The census is the thing the machine's been fighting with since Monday. It says the lane's empty. The machine says the census is what the jammers want us to believe. So the census is a fault, and the fault goes in the queue, and the queue goes to the operator, and the operator is—" he waved a hand at the ceiling, meaning the upper floors, the wing, the whole vertical building of procedure "—up there, clearing the faults in batches of forty, because the Office has decided that batches of forty are efficient. Did you see the Park Circus strike this morning?"

"No."

"Six structures. Four of them were empty. Two weren't." Ritwik's voice rose. "One of them was a school. A private one, in the lane behind the mosque. The model had classed it as a residence because somebody's cellular ghost was registered to it. The school had been closed since the summer. The ghost belonged to the caretaker's son. He's eleven." He stopped. His hands were shaking slightly, and he put them in his pockets. "The Office says the school was a *suspected training site*. They've got a note on the file. *Structure contained an unregistered gathering consistent with jamming signature.* It was a boy and his grandmother having their breakfast."

Apoorba said nothing. He was looking at the terminal. The file for Park Circus was on it now, in the overflow queue, with its header and its correlates and its composite index, which had been revised twice since the morning, as though the machine had been changing its mind about the boy's breakfast in real time.

"Why are you telling me?" he asked.

Ritwik looked at him then, properly, for the first time. His face was pale and young and frightened. "Because you declined the dog," he said. "On the fourth day. Everybody upstairs knows. They say you're the one who got the Chatterji wing a reputation for noticing things." He swallowed. "I don't want to notice things anymore. I want someone to tell me it's a fault in the classifier and not a fault in me."

"It's a fault in the classifier," said Apoorba quietly.

"Is it?"

"It's a fault in the people who built the classifier to see a shape in every gap," said Apoorba, and heard his own voice with a clarity that surprised him. "And a fault in the people who wrote the handbook that says silence is a fault. And a fault in the men who confirm files with twenty seconds and then go home to dinner. And a fault in me, for the eleven months I was one of them." He pressed his palms flat against the desk, feeling the fan's vibration. "But it isn't yours, Ritwik. Not the way they want you to think."

Ritwik stared at him for a long moment. Then, without a word, he gathered his stack of overflow and went back up the stairs. Apoorba heard the steel door close behind him, and the cold clean chime of the card reader, and then nothing.

He sat very still.

Across the chamber, Mrs. Dutta had not looked up. But her lips were not moving. She had her hand flat on the cover of a register, and she was looking at the brass bell on her desk, and when Apoorba caught her eye she did not look away.

"Mr. Chatterji," she said quietly. "You've got a boy in Park Circus who's eleven."

"I don't know the boy."

"No. But you know his grandmother's breakfast." She picked up the bell and turned it in her fingers, the way she had done every day for eleven days, and then, very deliberately, set it down in front of her and pushed it an inch toward him across the cold metal of the desk. "My son was called Arnab. He was twenty-three. He worked in the registration office on Chetla Road, until the directorate asked for the reconciliation staff to be moved to the Strand. He sent me a message the night before he was taken. It said the register had a woman's name on a phone that had been switched off for five years, and the name was the same as a woman he'd been at school with, and he didn't know whether to tell anyone." She folded her hands. "He didn't tell anyone. He was taken anyway. They said it was a conspiracy with the Bally camp."

Apoorba did not move. He was thinking of Ashoke's biscuit tin, which he had never seen, and of the names read out on the bulletins, the arrested and the dead and the officially regretted.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." Mrs. Dutta's voice did not change. "There's a man called Ashoke in Lake Town who keeps a reel of tape in a biscuit tin. He used to read the bulletins. He was on the air until the reorganisation. He knew Arnab's voice, because Arnab once did the children's programme on the Saturday station, and Ashoke was the man who played the music between the stories." She paused. "Ashoke has a friend called Malati. Malati teaches children at the old municipal school in Entally, or she did until they closed it. She still has a telephone that works on the mesh. The number is on the back of this bell."

Apoorba picked up the bell and turned it over. On its base, scratched with a fine point, were ten digits and the word *Entally*.

"Why would you give me this?" he asked.

Mrs. Dutta looked at him for a long time over the top of her spectacles. "Because you're the only operator in the wing," she said, "who has ever once tried to put a name on a card. And because I have been counting the hours since Monday, Mr. Chatterji, and every one of them has been a fault. I am tired of being the only person in this building who knows what a loop looks like with no floor under it."

---

Ritwik had never been to Park Circus on foot. He had been there twice in a car, both times on directorate business, both times with the window up and the driver's eyes fixed on the road. Now he walked the lanes behind the mosque in the grey hour after the strike, with his hands in the pockets of his cheap jacket and his shoes slipping on the wet brick, and the first thing he noticed was the smell. It was not smoke. Smoke he had expected. It was paper, burnt paper, the thin bitter smell of exercise books and ledgers and the printed sheets of a school's register, and under it the sweet heavy smell of something cooking that nobody was going to eat.

The school was called Anwar Memorial Primary. It had a green gate that had been painted over so many times the lettering had gone soft, and a bougainvillea over the entrance that had been allowed to grow wild, and two storeys of pale yellow walls that had been struck on the north side, so that the upper floor sagged into the ground floor like a cake that had fallen in the oven. A fire crew from the municipal station was damping down the rubble with hoses that ran in black channels down the lane. Nobody was shouting. The men moved slowly, with the flat resignation of people who had been to too many of these, and when one of them saw Ritwik's badge he looked at it for a long moment and then looked away.

Ritwik was not supposed to be there. The overflow files had been routed to the wing by the classifier, and the wing had sent the strike confirmation down to the archive with a note that read *Park Circus cluster — residential — civilian count pending.* He had been sent up to the wing that morning with a stack of printed correlates to hand over to the reconciliation cell, and the reconciliation cell had asked him, very politely, whether he would mind taking a look at the damage report for the file on his way back down, because nobody on the upper floors wanted to be seen reading it.

So he had come. He had told himself he was checking the census. He had found, when he reached the gate, that he was checking something else.

A woman was sitting on the kerb across the lane, outside a shuttered tailor's shop. She was perhaps sixty, in a faded cotton sari with the end drawn over her head, and she had a small boy asleep across her knees. The boy's cap had fallen off, and his hair was grey with dust. Beside her on the pavement sat an old man in a white vest with a bandage around his skull, holding a bundle of schoolbooks in both arms. The books were blackened on their edges. He was not reading them. He was pressing them against his chest, very gently, the way a man holds something he has been asked to bring to someone who is not yet ready to take it.

Ritwik stopped in front of them. He did not know what to say. He had a badge in his pocket and a notebook in his jacket, and the notebook had a column in it for *civilian count* that the reconciliation cell had asked him to fill in. He looked at the old man's bandage. He looked at the grey hair on the boy's head.

"Are you from the municipality?" the woman asked. She did not raise her eyes.

"No," said Ritwik. "From the Strand."

She nodded slowly, as though this were the answer she had been expecting. "The caretaker's son," she said. "Shahid. He's eleven. He's in the hospital at Park Circus. They said he had a broken leg and a burn. He was asleep on the top floor with his grandmother, because the caretaker's room is up there, under the roof. Nobody told us the school had been closed. They said it was a training site."

"I know," said Ritwik. His voice came out thin.

The woman lifted her head then, slowly, and looked at him. Her eyes were red-rimmed and perfectly dry. "You know," she said. "Do you?"

He did not answer. He took out the notebook and opened it to the column. Under *civilian count* the reconciliation cell had typed, in advance, the word *pending*. He had written *two*. Then he had crossed out the *two* and written *three*, because the grandmother was in the hospital too, and then he had crossed out the *three*, because he had remembered the little girl who lived in the flat above the tailor's, who had been on the stairs when the first shell hit and who had not come home from the hospital at all.

He had not written her name. He did not know it.

"What are you going to write?" the woman said.

"I don't know."

"You're going to write what the file says."

"Yes," said Ritwik. "I think so."

"Then write it in your own hand," said the woman, "so that when you go back up those stairs and somebody asks you what you saw, you'll know it was your hand that wrote it." She shifted the sleeping boy on her knees. "My husband was a clerk too, once. At the water board. He used to say that a number was only a sum that had forgotten who it was counting."

Ritwik stood on the wet brick with the notebook open in his hand. Behind the gate the fire crew had begun to pull a sheet of corrugated metal from the rubble, and it came away with a long screech, and under it, for a moment, was a schoolroom. A row of small wooden desks, all facing the same way. A blackboard with a chalked sum half rubbed out. A child's sandal on the floor, lying on its side, very neat.

He closed the notebook. He did not write anything more in it.

He walked back toward the main road with his hands in his pockets and his face wet, and at the corner he stopped, because a boy was standing there alone, holding a school bag against his chest, watching the fire crew with an expression that Ritwik recognised, because he had seen it for twenty-two years in his own face in the mirror of the lift at the Strand. It was the look of a child who had been told a thing was over and did not believe it.

"Are you going home?" Ritwik asked.

The boy looked at him. "I'm waiting for my mother."

"Where is she?"

"At the hospital." The boy's voice was flat. "Shahid's mother. She said to wait here so she'd know where to find me."

Ritwik stood beside him in silence. After a while he took his badge out of his pocket, turned it over, and looked at the photograph on the back of it, the photograph of a man who was not quite him, taken three years earlier in the wing's front hall with the directorate's blue flag behind him. Then he put the badge away.

"I'll wait with you," he said.

They waited together until the boy's mother came up the lane, grey-faced, carrying a plastic bag of medicines. Ritwik did not stay to be thanked. He went back to the main road and found a rickshaw and gave the driver his fare to the Strand, and sat in the back with the notebook shut on his knee, and did not open it again until he reached the chamber under the river wall.

When he did open it, he wrote one line, in his own hand, in the column marked *civilian count*. He wrote *Anwar Memorial Primary: one child, eleven, Shahid. One child, unnamed, seven or eight, tailor's flat, above the shop. One grandmother, age not recorded.*

Then, after a long pause, he wrote the name of the boy who had been waiting at the corner, because the boy had given it to him as he got into the rickshaw, shouting over the engine, and he had not been able to get it out of his head.

*Dilip.*

He sat looking at it for a long time. Then he took the notebook up the stairs to the wing, past the card reader and the cork board and the security desk, and he laid it open on the reconciliation cell's desk in front of a supervisor who did not look up, and he said, in a voice that went up at the end and did not come down: "The count is wrong. I've corrected it. Please file it under my name."

---

The old municipal school at Entally had been a red-brick building with a cracked bell tower and long windows that let in the light from the east in the early mornings. It had closed in 2042, when the children were moved into the directorate's cellular classrooms, which had no windows and no walls and were, the directorate's brochures said, *boundless in their reach*. The building had been left to the damp and the pigeons. Its bell had been taken down and sold for scrap. Its blackboards remained, though, in every classroom, running the full length of the walls, the green slate cracked and chalked over with the ghosts of a hundred lessons: long division, the Bengali alphabet, a sketch of the Hooghly with a boat drawn at the centre, a rough map of the eastern wards with a red line running through it, and in the top corner, in a child's careful capitals, the words *WE ARE NOT NUMBERS*.

Apoorba sat at a child's desk in the front row, under the blackboard, with his knees pressed up against the lid. The seat had been made for someone three feet shorter than him. He had come straight from the archive, through the lanes of Entally in the late afternoon, with the signed transfer notice still folded in his jacket and a steel tiffin box in his hand that he had not thought to leave behind. He had told Mitali he was working late. It was the first lie he had told her in eleven years of marriage that he had not immediately regretted.

The door at the back of the room opened.

Sucharita came in first. She was dressed in a plain cotton sari the colour of dust, and her hair was pinned back, and she had the stillness of a woman who had been awake for a very long time and had decided, somewhere between dawn and now, to stop being surprised by anything. Behind her came Malati, carrying a battery lamp, and then a broad-shouldered man with a burn scar on one forearm, who went straight to the window and stood looking out at the lane, his back to the room. Last came Ashoke, with his notebook in his shirt pocket and his small round face anxious and watchful, and he did not look at Apoorba at all. He went to the teacher's desk, sat down behind it, and folded his hands.

No one sat down beside the front row. No one spoke.

Sucharita walked up the centre aisle and stopped in front of the child's desk. She looked down at Apoorba, and then past him at the blackboard, at the sketch of the Hooghly and the boat and the words in the corner.

"You're the archivist," she said.

"Yes."

"Mrs. Dutta's bell rang on the mesh an hour ago. It's a crude thing, but it works. It said *Entally, the front row, the man who declined the dog.*" Her voice was flat. "You're late for the dog, Mr. Chatterji. The dog was a week ago. The boy who was counting on the stairs at Lansdowne was a week before that. You've had a long time to decline things."

"I've declined two," said Apoorba. "The dog and the boy's breakfast. I haven't confirmed a file in eleven days."

"Haven't you?"

He looked at her. He wanted to tell her the truth, and found that the truth had a shape he could not get his mouth around, a shape with a tailor's shop in it and a woman's laugh and a green door, and a line in a register that said *operator APC-4417* beside a time when he had been at his kitchen table with his hand over his mouth. He said, very carefully, "I've confirmed four hundred and nine files since the thirteenth. I did not look at any of them. My review interval for the first six weeks was twenty point one seconds, and it was entirely accurate. I was looking for the one thing the form asked for, and I found it every time, because the form had already decided what it was."

The room was silent. The man at the window had not turned round, but his shoulders had gone tight.

"Male," said Sucharita. "You were checking for male."

"Yes."

"Did you ever once hear a woman's voice and confirm her as a man?"

Apoorba closed his eyes. He saw the tap running in the kitchen. He heard the laugh, short and surprised, a small thing gone right unexpectedly. He opened his eyes.

"Yes," he said. "On the thirteenth. The file was SR-7. I was asked to confirm, and I didn't decline it. I confirmed it, and then I went home and ate nothing, and in the morning the machine had found a second file on the same handset, and I confirmed that too." He breathed in. "The woman was Rekha Mondal. She's alive. She carried her mother down the stairs of Lansdowne. I know because I read the post-strike cell's notes, the ones that were never meant to be read, on the Tuesday after. The cell didn't count her. The cell didn't count anybody on the third floor, because the strike was reassigned, and the reassignment was a ghost, and a ghost doesn't have a casualty field."

"And the strike on Lane Four?" said Malati, from the back of the room. Her voice was very quiet. "Was that you?"

"No. That was the machine finding a relay it hadn't been told about, in a room where a girl had a burn on her arm." He swallowed. "The operator who approved it was me. The approval came in at 22:14 from APC-4417, and I was at home at 22:14 with my wife's hand on my back. Somebody used my number. I found it in the register on the fourth day, and I logged it, and the log came back to me as a reliable component's reconciliation error. Nobody has asked me about it since." He looked at Sucharita. "I think that's the thing I'm here to say. Somebody is building the machine to make me the signature on every file. And I can't stop being the signature. I can only stop being the one who's fooled by it."

Sucharita did not move. She was looking at him with the expression he had seen once before, in the glass room at Salt Lake, on the face of a junior analyst who had just understood the arithmetic and did not yet know whether to be sick.

"Why now?" she said.

"Because the decoys are real," said Apoorba. "Because you built the red route and the blue, and somebody upstairs saw the pattern in the silences and decided that the silences were a jammer, and then decided that a jammer was a lane, and then decided that a lane was a school. And because the Office has a protocol for this." He took a breath. "They call it *Cascade*. It's in a folder in the wing that nobody's been allowed to read but the Director-General's office and two colonels. It's an automatic response to a loss of coherence in the classifier. If the model can't discriminate, Cascade takes the operator out of the loop. It authorises strike packages without a human confirmation for any cluster where the silence exceeds a threshold, and it runs on a timer. A countdown. It's not a fault. It's designed to clear a city fast."

Malati had come forward from the back of the room. She stood beside the teacher's desk with her hand on the battery lamp. "How fast?"

"I don't know. I've only seen the header. It's got a field for the timer." He did not look away. "The field is blank. The protocol has a dependency on the classifier's current threshold. So as long as the silences keep growing, the timer will keep getting shorter. Ritwik says the Park Circus strike was authorised in four seconds. That's because the operator was clearing a batch of forty. Under Cascade it wouldn't need an operator at all."

The room was very quiet. The man at the window turned round at last. He had a hard, careful face, and his eyes went immediately to Sucharita, and then to Apoorba, and then back to her, with the look of a man who had spent his life waiting for the moment when someone in a room decided whether to trust a stranger.

"Dipak," said Sucharita, without looking at him. "Do you believe him?"

"I believe the timer exists," said Dipak. "I don't believe he's brave. But he's read the header, and he's got the register number, and he's sitting in a child's desk in Entally with his knees up under his chin." He shrugged. "That's not nothing, Didi."

"No," said Sucharita. "It's not."

She came down the aisle and sat on the edge of the desk beside him, very close, so that he could smell the dust and the sandalwood soap and the faint bitter ink of the chalk on the blackboard behind her. Her face was closer than it had been at Salt Lake, and older, and entirely without anger, and that was harder to bear than any anger he had ever imagined.

"Here's what we want," she said quietly. "We want the register. Every handset in the eastern wards, the owners, the tower handshakes, the census corrections. We want the register so we can make the decoys walk the right streets and not the wrong ones. We want the Cascade header and the timer field. We want to know the threshold the protocol is waiting for, and we want it before the silences cross it. And we want you to keep being the operator who declines." Her eyes did not leave his. "That's going to cost you. You know that."

"I know."

"Do you? Because when they find the register has been opened from your chamber, they'll take your family's name off the list of people who can go to the lake in the winter. They'll take the transfer notice you signed and make it a confession. They'll take your daughter's school place. They'll call you a traitor in the wing's internal bulletin, and your wife will read it at the kitchen table on the same morning she packs your tiffin, and she won't know whether to put the second box in or leave it on the shelf." She paused. "Do you understand what I'm saying?"

"Yes."

"Then here's what I'm asking in return," said Sucharita. "Three conditions. You'll give us the register. You'll give us Cascade's header. You'll tell us the moment the Office moves the timer off the silence and onto anything else." She held up a finger. "And there will be no explosives in any civilian sector, under any circumstances, on any night. Not the tower. Not the depot. Not a relay box. Not the charges Tarak has been sitting on in a fishing hut since the eleventh."

"Agreed," said Apoorba.

"Two."

"What's two?"

"Sector Four stays excluded," said Sucharita. "The Data Desert boundary. East of the Dhapa embankment. The map on the back of the decoy plan. Nothing goes in there, not a handset, not a ghost, not a tailor's son's photograph, not a dog. If the machine looks at that silence and asks what it is, you tell it nothing. You confirm a fault and nothing else."

Apoorba was silent. He thought of the pencil loop on the back of the map, drawn carefully around an empty area of paddy and reed. He thought of the word *excluded* in a small slanted hand, and of a house at the edge of the water with a tin roof and a line of blue tarpaulin, and of a woman in a faded sari standing at a clay stove. He did not know her name. He knew she had a stove. He knew, from the register, that her house had never once registered a residential signal since the spring of 2041.

"Why?" he asked.

"Because a machine that can't see a place," said Sucharita, "will always, sooner or later, decide that the place is the thing it has been looking for."

She said it in the flat, careful tone of a woman quoting a sentence she had learned from someone else. Apoorba looked at her face and saw, very briefly, something move behind it, a flicker of the same grief he had seen on Ritwik's face, and on Mrs. Dutta's, and in his own reflection in the mirrored lift at Strand Road.

"Agreed," he said.

"Three," said Sucharita.

She did not hold up a finger this time. She looked at the blackboard instead, at the sketch of the Hooghly with its single boat, and at the words in the top corner in a child's careful capitals.

"Three is the one you won't like," she said quietly. "When the machine starts to make its own mistakes, the mistakes will become its judgments. You'll have to decide, every hour, which of them to let stand. And every time you let one stand, some family will be on the wrong side of your decision and you won't ever be told their names." She met his eyes. "You'll have to carry the names yourself. Not in the register. In your head. Every night, until it's over. Can you do that?"

Apoorba thought of the green book in his desk drawer at home, and the page of multiplication folded inside it under the foreword. He thought of Tuli writing the answer again in the same place, carefully, so that her mistake would be seen and not hidden.

"Yes," he said.

Sucharita stood. She looked down at him for a moment longer, and her expression did not soften, exactly, but something in it settled, the way water settles in a bucket that has been shaken.

"Mrs. Dutta's bell," she said. "Keep it. Ring it when you need us. Malati will hear it on the mesh."

"And if I can't ring it?"

"Then we'll come to Chamber Three ourselves," said Sucharita. "And you'll have to explain to the register why a woman in a cotton sari has been walking through the Strand basement at six in the evening, asking for the overflow queue by name."

Behind her, by the window, Dipak made a short sound that might have been a laugh. Ashoke, at the teacher's desk, had taken out his notebook and was writing something in it very slowly, in a neat round hand. Malati had put the battery lamp down on the desk and was looking at Apoorba with a expression he could not read, the expression of a teacher who has been handed a child's test paper and has decided, after all, to mark it kindly.

"Mr. Chatterji," she said. "Do you know what the children wrote on the board, before the school closed?"

He looked up at the blackboard. At the words in the corner.

*WE ARE NOT NUMBERS.*

"Yes," he said.

"Good," said Malati. "Then you've already done the first lesson. The rest is arithmetic."

---

The steps of the old Entally school ran down from the front door to the lane in four worn stone treads, and the lowest of them had sunk so far into the mud that the rainwater pooled there and never quite drained. Apoorba sat on the second step with his back against the iron railing and his shoes in the damp. He had come back to the schoolroom alone after the others had gone, and had found that he could not yet go home, so he sat in the dusk and watched the lane fill with the ordinary noise of Entally at seven in the evening: a bicycle bell, two women arguing over the price of fish, a radio playing a film song at a volume that suggested the owner was very old or very deaf. A child was practising the alphabet somewhere behind a half-open shutter. *Ka, kha, ga, gha.* The voice rose and fell, unhurried, and once it stopped, and started again from the beginning.

Sucharita came out of the school door behind him without a sound. He knew it was her because of the sandal-soap smell, and because she sat down on the step beside him without asking, her knees drawn up under the cotton sari, a flask in one hand and two small clay cups in the other.

"Malati left it on the desk," she said. "She says it's the last of the good tea from the Darjeeling stall, and that if I drink it all she will have me sent to the mesh's internal court."

She poured. The tea was dark and smelled of smoke. She handed him a cup. Her fingers did not quite touch his.

They drank in silence for a while. The child behind the shutter reached *gha* again and stopped, and the silence after it was thick and warm.

"The Park Circus strike," said Apoorba at last. "Ritwik came to the archive this afternoon. He said they had a count of three civilian deaths and a school with no name in the register."

"It has a name," said Sucharita. "Anwar Memorial Primary. Malati has the boy's name. Shahid. He's in the hospital at Park Circus with a broken leg. His grandmother is in the next bed. They're both alive."

"And the tailor's girl?"

Sucharita was quiet for a moment. "Malati doesn't know her name yet," she said. "She will by morning."

Apoorba turned the cup in his hands. He could feel the clay's heat through his palms. "I used to think the register was the thing that kept count," he said. "The census, the handshakes, the tower codes. Then I thought it was the classifier, because the classifier was what I was told to trust. Now I think it's just a list of the things people forgot to tell us about each other. Ritwik said the school had been closed since summer. The census had the caretaker's room registered as a residence. A boy who'd been living there for six months had never once been asked to register. The register said he didn't exist. So the machine didn't count him as anything except a ghost, and a ghost is a thing you can bomb without anyone asking afterward whether it had a grandmother."

"Yes," said Sucharita. "That's what a register does."

"You built the decoys to make the register lie more."

"I built them to make the register too busy to tell the truth," she said, without heat. "It's not the same thing. It's worse, in its way. A lie is something you can be held to. A flood of contradictions just becomes weather." She looked down at her tea. "I didn't think about the caretaker's room. I should have. Dipak walked the Phoolbagan lane for me the next morning, with his torch, and counted the windows. He counted sixteen rooms with someone sleeping in them that the census had listed as empty. I'd made the resolution a thousand times coarser than the lane. I'd made the model see the tailor's shop and not the girl on the floor."

"And you're going to tell the Office that?"

"I'm going to tell Malati," said Sucharita, "and Malati will tell Ashoke, and Ashoke will read it into his tape with the names of the dead, in his announcer's voice, so that somebody in Lake Town will hear it who isn't in any file." She set her cup down on the step. "The Office won't hear. The Office doesn't need to hear. That's the thing I keep coming back to, Mr. Chatterji. The whole machine was built so that nobody has to hear."

"Apoorba," he said.

She looked at him.

"You can call me Apoorba. You've been calling me Mr. Chatterji since the first night, and I'm tired of being the man in the file." He turned the cup again. "My wife calls me Pori. My daughter calls me Baba, when she wants something, and Mahim when she thinks I'm not listening. Shibnath Ghosh called me Mahim at the tank in Tollygunge. I haven't been Apoorba to anybody in months."

Sucharita was silent for a long moment. Then she said, very quietly, "Sucharita."

"I know."

"Everyone knows my name. The Office has it on the defector's file in a folder with a red tab." She picked up the flask and poured herself the last of the tea, which was cold now and bitter. "I was called Bharati by my mother, because she liked the novel and wanted a daughter who would be stubborn. Gourmohan calls me Sucharita-di, because he is the only man I've ever met who thinks an older sister is a moral position. Malati calls me Chhoto-di, because I'm the youngest in the mesh and she is sure I'll never be the oldest. Tarak calls me nothing at all."

"Why nothing?"

"Because he doesn't trust me," said Sucharita. "He's right not to. I came from the cartel. I wrote a sentence in four seconds. I wasn't ever sure it was a betrayal, when I wrote it, because I thought the numbers were the only thing that could be honest." She set the flask on the step. "I'm not sure now that anything is honest, except the child reading her alphabet behind that shutter. Listen to her. She's stopped at *gha* again."

They listened. The voice came up from the shutter, very small and clear, and reached *gha*, and stopped, and then, with a small impatient sigh that could have come from any child in any century, began again from *ka*.

"What's her name?" asked Apoorba.

"I don't know. Malati would."

"I'd like to know."

"Then ask her in the morning, when the mesh is quiet," said Sucharita. "Tell her you're the man who declined the dog, and she'll give you the name for nothing." Her mouth moved, not quite a smile. "She's very fond of people who decline things. She says it's the only thing a clerk can do that the machine can't mistake for a fault."

He looked at her. The light from the lane had gone pale. He could see the shape of her face, the high cheekbones and the stubborn set of the jaw, and the lines at the corners of her eyes that had not been there in the glass room at Salt Lake, and the faint white mark on the inside of her wrist where she had once, he would learn later from Malati, tried to cut herself on the night she first understood what the Ballygunge memo had cost, and had stopped halfway through because a junior analyst had come in with a cup of tea and asked her, quite cheerfully, whether she wanted sugar.

"My daughter," he said.

"Tuli."

He looked up sharply. "You know her name?"

"It's in your personnel file. The Office's notes on your domestic situation. *Child, eight, school place secured at Tollygunge Girls'. Wife, teacher of Bengali, on part-time leave.*" She said it flatly, without apology. "I read your file on the first night, Apoorba. It's what we did. We read everyone's files until we found the one line that made them human."

"And what did you find in mine?"

"The multiplication," said Sucharita. "I didn't know it was there until Malati told me about the page. Your wife writes the answer in full. And your daughter writes it again in the same place." She looked at him directly. "That was the line I kept thinking about. That somebody in your house had decided a mistake should be shown, and not hidden. It doesn't make the machine any more honest. But it made me want to go and see the people who do that."

Apoorba's throat was tight. He drank the rest of the cold tea, though it burned.

"She got twelve times fourteen wrong," he said. "The morning of the strike. She wrote one hundred and fifty-eight. Mitali wrote the answer in full on a page from her exercise book and put it in my tiffin box, the second one, the small one, the one I never opened on the metro." He set the clay cup down on the step very carefully. "I opened it that night. I've kept the page inside the cover of a book the Director-General wrote. I didn't know why until last week."

"Why?"

"Because it's the only sentence in the whole directorate that's ever been corrected in the same place it was made," he said. "Everything else is written once, and confirmed in twenty seconds, and never looked at again."

Sucharita did not answer. She sat beside him on the wet stone with her hands folded in her lap and her face turned toward the lane, where the fish-sellers were packing up their baskets and the bicycle bell rang once more and then fell silent. After a long time she said, very softly, not to him exactly:

"My mother used to do the accounts for the church in Ballygunge. For the Christian school. She'd sit at the kitchen table with the ledger and a red pencil and check every column twice, and if one figure was wrong she'd rub it out with her thumb and write it again, very neatly, in the same place. I thought it was the most boring thing in the world. I used to beg her to let me do it." Her voice did not change. "She sent me a birthday card last year. Through a cousin in Pune. It had a line of Hemanta on the front and a sum on the back, in her red pencil. Fourteen times twelve. She'd written the answer and then, underneath it, in brackets, *check*."

Apoorba turned to look at her. Her face in the lane's last light was perfectly still, and her eyes were dry, and her hands were gripping each other in her lap so hard that the knuckles had gone white.

"Did you check?" he asked.

"I did it four times," said Sucharita. "I got a hundred and sixty-eight every time. I don't know what I was expecting."

They sat on the step in the dark. Behind them, through the half-open door of the school, the blackboard was a faint grey sheet in the last of the light, and the sketch of the Hooghly with its single boat was still there, and the words in the top corner in a child's careful capitals.

"Apoorba," said Sucharita at last.

"Yes?"

"The timer. The Cascade box. When you're back in the chamber tomorrow, I want you to look at the count on the overflow queue at six in the evening, and write it on the back of Mrs. Dutta's bell with a pencil. Not in the register. On the bell." She stood, and smoothed her sari, and picked up the empty flask. "Every evening. Until it stops."

"Why on the bell?"

"Because the register resets at six in the morning," said Sucharita, "and the bell is the only thing in that building nobody has ever thought to erase."

She went back up the school steps and into the dark. He heard the door close behind her, and then her footsteps going down the hall, and then nothing. He sat a while longer on the cold stone with the empty clay cup in his hand, and then he took out his phone, the cheap one Malati had given him, and looked at the screen, where a single message from an unknown number had just arrived.

*Shahid's grandmother is awake. She asked whether the man who sang at the roof had come back to Entally. Malati says to tell you yes.*

He did not know the man who sang at the roof. He thought he did. He typed nothing in reply. He put the phone in his pocket, beside the brass bell, and walked home through the lanes of Entally, and for the first time in twenty days he did not count the steps.

At six the next evening the archive's overflow counter reset itself, as the register said it would, and then, a minute later, climbed again from zero.

Apoorba watched it do this from the green vinyl chair. He had been watching the counter since noon. The Priority A queue had come down to the chambers in waves through the afternoon, each wave a grey block of overflow files with the directorate's blue seal in the header, and each file a silence that the classifier had decided was a residence, and each residence a line in a grid that had been labelled, on the wing's internal map, *Dhapa Boundary — Sector 4 — Active*. The grid had grown. At three o'clock it covered a single cell. By five it covered four, and the cells had begun to show, in the small grey fields beside them, a count that was no longer a count of structures.

*Population signal — adjusted.*

He had stopped reading the figures at four. He read them now, at six, because the bell was on the desk in front of him, and because Sucharita had said *every evening until it stops*, and he had found that the bell was a thing that needed a number written on it to justify itself.

He took a pencil from the drawer. It was a cheap wooden pencil with a blunt lead, the kind the archive issued for overflow tickets, and it was the first thing he had ever written on a piece of directorate property that was not a signature. He held the bell in his left hand, turned so that its base faced him, and under the ten digits and the word *Entally*, in small clear numerals, he wrote the figure from the grid's top right cell.

*1,412.*

He looked at it for a long time. Then he wrote, beside it, very small, the time: *18:00*.

Across the chamber, Mrs. Dutta had not looked up from her register. But her lips had stopped moving. She had her spectacles off and folded in her lap, and her hand was resting on the grey card cover of a book that was not the register, a book with eleven pages, which Apoorba had not returned to the Director-General's shelf and had not been asked to return. She had told him the first evening, without any ceremony, that she had made a copy of the eleventh page on the archive's old photocopier, and had hidden the copy in the bottom of a box of ledgers from the water board, and that she would be very surprised if anyone in the wing ever went looking for a water-board ledger.

"Is it still the same number?" she asked, without raising her head.

"No," said Apoorba. "It's gone up by eighty."

"Then they've counted the road again."

"They've counted the people who walked back onto it this afternoon," he said. "Malati's mesh says three hundred and forty were there at dawn and seven hundred by noon. The machine doesn't know what to do with a crowd that has decided to stay in one place. It keeps asking whether it's a residence. And every time it asks, the answer goes up."

Mrs. Dutta was silent for a moment. Then she said, in a voice so quiet he almost did not hear it over the fan: "My son would have written the number on the back of the register, Mr. Chatterji. In the margin. So that the next person who opened the book would have to see it."

"I know."

"He thought a clerk's job was to make the machine look at what it had done." She put her spectacles back on. "He was wrong about that. A machine doesn't look at anything. But the clerk who has to sign for it does."

Apoorba turned the bell in his hand. The pencil figure was already smudging a little where his thumb had rested on it. He would have to write it again tomorrow, in the same place, because the old one would be rubbed off by the bell's own weight on the desk, and because Sucharita had been very clear that the number was not to be copied into anything the directorate could read. The bell was not a record. It was a reminder. A child's school bell, scratched with a ledger's pencil, carried in a clerk's pocket, that rang only when someone chose to ring it.

He picked up the terminal's handset and dialled Malati's number from memory, the ten digits he had learned on the first night and had not once written down. It rang twice.

"Tell Sucharita," he said, when the line opened, "the bell says one thousand, four hundred and twelve, at six. Up eighty since noon. And the Park Circus cell has stopped counting the grandmother as a civilian and started counting her as a witness." He stopped. "Tell her the man who declined the dog is still at his desk."

There was a long silence on the line. Then Malati said, very dryly: "She knows. She's been sitting on the front step of the school since five, and she's been saying your name to the lane as if it were a sum she was checking."

She hung up.

Apoorba put the handset down. He sat back in the green vinyl chair, which creaked, and looked up at the white disc of the clock above the steel door, and at the grid on the terminal beside him, and at the small grey number that had just, while he was not looking, changed from *1,412* to *1,413*.

One more. One more name the machine had decided was a house.

He took the pencil and wrote it on the bell, beside the first figure, without erasing anything. Two numbers, one above the other, in a clerk's blunt hand. He did not know yet whether the second one would be a family or a fault. He knew only that for the first time since the thirteenth of October, a number he had been asked to confirm had been written down, in his own hand, by the one man in the building who was required to look at it.

Mrs. Dutta turned a page. Her lips began again to move. The fan went on turning above the steel door. And at 18:01, across the chamber, in the corner of the terminal where the overflow counter had been reset and had begun, very slowly, to climb, a small green box appeared with a single line of text that nobody had asked for.

*Operator APC-4417: review interval exceeded. Non-confirmation event logged. Please confirm or decline within the standard interval to avoid compliance referral.*

He read it, and felt nothing. He reached out, very calmly, and pressed the amber key.

CHAPTER 6.

On the nineteenth day of the decoy campaign, the classifier stopped asking whether a thing was there and began to ask instead whether it had been missed.

Sucharita understood it before the others did. She had been watching the mesh logs since dawn, on the battered tablet on the kitchen table in Mandeville Road, with a cup of tea going cold beside it and the window open to a courtyard full of wet laundry and the sound of a radio in a neighbour's flat playing a Hemanta song in the wrong key. The logs were a column of timestamps and cluster identifiers, each one a silence, each silence a flag, each flag a line in the classifier's queue. She had grown used to reading them the way one reads weather. Most mornings the column was long and grey and sluggish, with the clusters coming in at intervals of several minutes, and the operators in the wing clearing them one by one, slowly, with their twenty seconds and their dinners to get to.

On the nineteenth morning the column had changed shape.

The intervals had shrunk. Three minutes became ninety seconds, and then forty, and then eleven. The clusters had begun to appear in pairs, and then in threes, and the operators' clearance times, which she could see in the right-hand margin, had dropped from twenty seconds to four, and then to one. At 07:14 there were eighty-three flags in the queue. At 07:16 there were two hundred and ten. At 07:19 the column ran off the bottom of the screen and she had to scroll it, and the scroll bar was a thin white sliver at the edge of the display, and her hand on the mouse was no longer steady.

"Gourmohan," she said.

He was at the sink, washing two cups. He had come in from Dhapa at dawn with the last of the decoy handsets in a canvas bag and a rough bandage across the knuckles of his right hand where a dog had bitten him on the boundary road. He turned round at her voice, wiping his hands on a towel, and came to the table, and looked over her shoulder at the screen.

He did not say anything for a long time.

"They've stopped reading the silences," he said at last. "They're reading the gaps between them."

"What does that mean?"

"It means the classifier has started to look for the thing the decoys are supposed to fill." He put his finger on the glass, very lightly, beside a cluster identifier that had appeared three times in the last minute. "Every decoy walks a route. Every route has a gap in it, where the handset stops, or turns, or goes dark for a moment when the battery swaps. The model's been treating those gaps as faults. Now it's treating them as places where something is hiding. It's looking for the thing that should be in the gap and isn't." He looked at her. "The silence has a shape. We taught it that. We gave it the red route, and the red route has a gap at every tea stall."

"And it's filling the gaps," said Sucharita, very quietly, "with strike packages."

"Yes."

"Then the decoys are feeding it."

"Yes," said Gourmohan. "They're feeding it so well it's stopped eating anything else."

On the tablet a new line appeared at the top of the column. It was a cluster identifier she did not recognise, a sequence of letters and digits beginning with the prefix *SEC4*, and beside it a status field that read, in the plain grey font of the classifier's public logs: *Silence — excessive — no residential registration — pattern: boundary.*

Sucharita stopped breathing.

"Gourmohan."

He had seen it too. He had gone very still beside her, one hand still on the table, the other hanging at his side with its bandaged knuckles tight.

"That's the boundary," he said, very softly. "That's Dhapa. That's—"

"It's the excluded area," said Sucharita. "We kept the decoys out. Every route, every lane. Malati checked the handsets twice. Dipak checked the carts. There's nothing in Sector Four that can make a signal." She heard her own voice rising and forced it down. "The classifier shouldn't be able to see it at all."

"It doesn't see it," said Gourmohan. "It sees the place it can't see. That's what Hallucination Suppression was built for. It's been told a silence is a fault. It's been told a fault has a shape. And the only place in the eastern wards with a silence big enough to have a shape is the one we drew on the map."

Sucharita looked at the cluster identifier. The status field had changed while they watched. It now read: *Silence — persistent — duration 00:41:12 — threshold crossed — Cascade pre-authorisation pending.*

She knew what Cascade was. Apoorba had told them in the schoolroom at Entally, in his careful flat archivist's voice, with his knees pressed up against the child's desk. *It's a countdown. It's designed to clear a city fast.* She had written the words on the back of the map, under *excluded*, in the small slanted hand, and had felt, at the time, a cold distant horror, the horror of a woman reading the specifications for a thing she had already helped to build.

"The timer," she said.

"Check the mesh," said Gourmohan. "Malati's got the Cascade header. Apoorba sent it at four this morning, through the bell. It's in her queue."

Sucharita pulled up the mesh's private channel. It took her three attempts, because her hands were no longer quite obeying her. At last the screen filled with a grey block of text that had been copied, by hand, from the directorate's archive terminal into a schoolroom slate and then photographed and sent through three relay nodes in twenty minutes. The handwriting in the photograph was small and round and very careful.

*CASCADE — AUTOMATED RESPONSE — LOSS OF DISCRIMINATION.*

*Trigger: silence threshold exceeded in any cluster for >60 seconds, classifier confidence <0.4.*

*Action: pre-authorise strike packages to cluster centroid. Operator confirmation waived. Timer initiated.*

*Timer: 02:00:00. Non-extendable. Expires: strike execution.*

*Override: Office of the Director-General (S.K.) only. Override code: [REDACTED]. Override window: duration of timer.*

Sucharita read the last line three times. Then she looked at the cluster identifier, and the duration field beside it, which had changed again.

*00:43:56.*

The timer had started at 06:35. It had started, she understood, at the moment the silence in Sector Four had crossed sixty seconds, a silence that she herself had made by building the boundary and keeping it empty, and then by refusing, in the schoolroom and on the bheri and at the stove in the house in Mandeville Road, to let anyone go near the excluded line. The silence had been the thing that kept the place safe. It had also been the thing that made it visible.

"We pull the decoys," said Gourmohan, behind her. His voice was very level. "Now. Every route. Every cart. The red and the blue. The machine will have nothing left to fill its gaps with except the boundary."

"If we pull them," said Sucharita, "it will see the gaps close. It will ask why. It will fill them with the nearest silence it can find, which is the boundary too."

"Then we fill the boundary ourselves."

"With what?"

"With people."

She looked up at him. He was standing very straight beside the table, his bandaged hand clenched, his face in the grey morning light the face of a man who had been waiting for a very long time to say a single sentence and had only now found out what it would cost him.

"Anandamoyi's house," said Sucharita. "Eleven hundred people sleeping on her floors, Gourmohan. Two hundred more on the boundary road. The old woman with the cough. The boy who counts to a hundred. The girl with the burn." She heard her own voice crack on the last word, and stopped. "If the machine decides the boundary is a residence, it doesn't need the census. It will count every person who's standing there. Every one. And the count will be the thing it bombs."

"I know," said Gourmohan.

"Then how do you fill a silence with people who are the thing the silence is hiding?"

He did not answer her. He reached past her, very carefully, with his good hand, and turned the tablet so that the cluster identifier faced him. He looked at the duration field for a long time. Then he took his phone from his pocket, a cheap handset with a cracked screen that Malati had given him after the funeral, and dialled a number, and held the phone to his ear, and waited.

"Ma," he said, when the line connected. His voice was perfectly calm. "I need you to listen to me. Don't ask me why. Just listen. Two hours. Start walking the boundary road. Everyone on the floors, everyone on the road, everyone in the sheds and the fishermen's huts. Tell them to walk toward the Chetla end, and to carry the children, and to keep singing."

There was a pause. In the pause Sucharita heard, very faintly, through the cheap phone's speaker, the sound of a ladle against an iron pot and a woman's voice, unhurried, saying something that Sucharita did not catch.

Then Anandamoyi said, quite clearly: "Gour. Who is going to carry the old ones?"

"I will."

"Then carry them properly," said Anandamoyi. "And come home before the light goes. I've made the khichuri."

The line went dead.

Gourmohan put the phone down on the table beside the tablet. He stood looking at it for a long moment. Then he said, to no one in particular, in a voice so quiet that Sucharita barely heard it over the radio in the neighbour's flat: "She's already started."

On the screen the duration field ticked over, one digit, then another, and the number beside the cluster identifier, the number that had been *00:43:56* a minute before, was now *01:58:12*.

It had not counted down. It had reset.

Sucharita stared at it. Then she understood, with the cold dreadful precision that came to her only when the arithmetic had already been done for her by somebody else. The machine had been waiting for the boundary to be filled. It had seen the first families walking out of the Dhapa settlement toward the Chetla road, a long slow column of people in the early light, and it had counted them, every one, and it had decided, on the basis of that count, that the silence had been broken by a residence. The timer had reset to the full two hours to allow for the new residence. It was not in a hurry. It did not need to be. It had only to wait for the living to stop walking and settle again into the place the census said was empty.

"The people aren't leaving," Sucharita said, very low. "They're going to the road. The road's a residence now. The machine's counted the road."

Gourmohan did not look at her. He was looking at the number in the duration field, the two hours, the figure that had reset while they watched and was ticking down now, slowly, in the flat grey font of a thing that had no reason to be quick.

*01:57:44.*

"Then we don't leave the road," said Gourmohan Roy. "We go to the people who wrote the timer."

---

The boundary road did not sleep. It had not slept since the morning, when the column of people had begun to walk along it from the Dhapa settlement toward the Chetla end, and then had stopped, and turned, and walked back. Nobody had been able to say, afterward, which of them had decided that the road was the place to be. Anandamoyi said it had been the old women. Malati said it had been the boy with the counting. Saikat said it had been the dog, who had lain down in the middle of the embankment at noon and would not get up, and whom everyone had been obliged to step around until the people behind them had simply sat down too.

By midnight there were three hundred and forty of them on the road, and perhaps two hundred more in the fishermen's huts along the water, and a fire had been lit at the midpoint, a long low bonfire of dried reeds and rice-straw and broken fish crates, and the smoke of it rolled slowly out over the bheris and lay on the surface of the water like a second, lower sky. The children had been put to sleep in the lee of the embankment on heaps of coats and sacking. The old people sat nearer the fire with their backs to the wind. The young women moved between the two groups with pots of lentils and water in their hands, and nobody gave orders, and nobody needed to.

Gourmohan had been on his feet for twenty hours. He had carried the old woman with the cough from the first hut to the second, and then back again, when she insisted on being taken to see the fire. He had carried a boy of four who had fallen asleep on the way and woken, halfway, and begun to scream, and had been soothed only when the big man with the bandaged hand began, very quietly, to sing the second verse of the river song into the back of his neck. He had taken the boy's mother's bundle, and her husband's place at the water-pot, and her shoulder when she stumbled on the broken paving at the Chetla end, and had let her cry into his shirt for a long time without saying anything at all.

He sat now on an upturned fish crate at the edge of the firelight, with his back to the embankment and his bandaged hand resting on his knee. The bandage had soaked through in two places. He had not changed it. He had been told, twice, by two different women, that a dog's bite that had not stopped weeping after three days was a thing to be seen by a doctor, and he had said yes both times and had not moved.

Across the fire, Anandamoyi was sorting lentils into a tin plate. She did it with her head bent and her fingers moving very fast, picking out the grit and the broken husks and the occasional black seed, and she did not look up.

"You're thinking about the roof," she said.

"Yes."

"You're thinking about the old man with the white hair, who used to sit on the crate and tell you to sit down before someone shot you for standing."

He had not told her that. He did not ask how she knew.

"Yes," he said.

"He was a bad drunk," said Anandamoyi, "and a good teacher, and he was in love with me for thirty-one years, and I never once let him say so, because I thought if I did, he'd stop singing with Mrs. Basu on Thursdays." She picked out another husk. "Mrs. Basu wasn't a good singer. Everyone knew it. She sang the way a woman climbs stairs when she's carrying a pot. But she never once missed a Thursday in forty years, and she taught you the second verse by putting your hand on the harmonium's lid so you could feel the note come up through the wood. I was there. I was at the door of the upper room with a pan of rice in my hands. I saw you cry."

"I don't remember crying."

"You were nine. You didn't want to be seen. You turned your face to the wall and pretended you had a stone in your eye." She set the tin plate down and looked at him at last across the firelight. "That's the boy I'm thinking of, Gour. Not the one who wrote the speech. The one who turned to the wall and didn't want Mrs. Basu to see him cry, and cried anyway, into the plaster."

He could not answer. He looked down at his bandaged hand. The blood had dried black along the edges of the cloth, and it had stuck the gauze to the skin, and when he moved his fingers it pulled.

"I put them on the roof," he said, very low. "I made them sing. I made them stand in the open and read the names, and the machine read them too, and then it read the lane, and then it read the house. Shibnath-da came down because I'd made him believe the radio was safe. Mrs. Basu went upstairs with the milk because the girl had a burn and I'd told her the ambulance was coming. I knew the number, Ma. I knew the number before I went up. Four point three. I knew it in the kitchen, and I went up anyway, and I thought I was carrying something that would make the numbers stop."

"And did they?"

"No."

"No," said Anandamoyi. "They never do." She picked up the plate again and tipped the lentils into a pot at her knee. "The numbers don't stop, Gour. They don't know how. They're only ever counted by someone who's already decided to be sorry. That's the difference between a number and a family. A family can sit down and be sorry together, and eat its dinner after."

Gourmohan looked across the fire at the children asleep on the coats, at the young woman with the bandaged foot, who was dozing with her head against the embankment and the boy on her lap, at the old woman with the cough, who was watching him over the rim of a tin cup with an expression he could not read.

"Ma," he said. "The timer. Cascade. It's counting again."

"I know. Malati told me."

"It's at ninety-one minutes. It stopped at thirty-one because someone declined the strike in the wing. It's climbing back toward two hours now. The machine doesn't think the road is empty. It thinks the road is a house it's just found out about. And every time the number goes up, it's going to ask the operators in the wing to confirm us as a residence."

"And what do you want the operators to do?"

He thought of the register in the archive. The eleventh page with its crossed-out names and its fountain-pen sentence underlined twice. *Override is a mercy.* He thought of Apoorba sitting in the green vinyl chair with his hands flat on the desk and a brass bell in his pocket, and of the fact that he had declined a strike at 18:50 and had not yet been removed from his post for it.

"I want them to see us," he said. "I want the wing to look at the screen, and see the count, and see that the number they've been given is three hundred and forty people on a road with a dog lying across it, and that the number is sitting in a box that says *Operator confirmation required*. I want somebody to read the box out loud."

"And if nobody does?"

"Then the road will have to be the thing they read," said Gourmohan. "And we'll be sitting on it when they do."

Anandamoyi smiled. It was a small, tired, entirely unsentimental smile, and it made her look for a moment almost exactly like the woman who had stood at the stove in the house on the boundary with her back to him, saying *count the families you feed*.

"Then sit down, Gour," she said. "Before someone shoots you for standing."

He sat. The fish crate was damp and cold through his trousers. Somewhere down the embankment a fishing boat's lamp had been lit, a small hard yellow point on the black water, and the wind had begun to turn, very slightly, so that the smoke of the fire leaned out over the reeds and then drew back toward them, and the sound of the sleeping children rose and fell with it like the breath of one large animal.

Above them, beyond the lowest of the clouds, a faint pale line of light was moving very slowly across the sky from the west. It was too steady to be a star. It was too high to be an aircraft. Gourmohan watched it for a long time, and then he saw, further along the ridge of the embankment, a second light, and then a third, moving in the same slow line, spaced evenly, like the beads of a rosary being told by someone who had all night.

Saikat came up the embankment from the water with his torn school tie in his hand and stood beside the fire, looking up at the lights too.

"Sensor sweep," he said. "The high ones. They're doing the boundary road again. The machine's never flown them this high before."

"How do you know?"

"Because Dipak's been counting them since sundown. He says they're three times the altitude they were last week, and they're not sweeping the Chetla end anymore. They're only sweeping the fence." Saikat's voice was flat. "He says the sensor's been turned to read silence. He says the silence is us."

Gourmohan did not move. He sat with his bandaged hand in his lap and his face turned up toward the slow pale lines in the dark, and he thought, quite clearly, of the sentence Sucharita had written at the bottom of the decoy map and had never shown him. *Do not let it learn this boundary.*

"It's learned it," he said.

"Then what do we do?"

Gourmohan looked at the boy. Saikat had grown again in the last three days, or perhaps it was only the firelight. He had the torn school tie knotted tight at the throat and his father's stubborn jaw and a look in his eyes that was not fear and not courage but a plain refusal to be the first to look away.

"We sing the second verse again," Gourmohan said, "and we let the sensors read it. And then we sit down. And then we eat."

"That's not a plan."

"No," said Gourmohan. "It's what a family does."

Saikat stared at him for a moment. Then, slowly, he sat down on the damp embankment beside the fire, pulled his knees up under his chin, and began, very quietly, to sing. His voice cracked on the high notes, the way it always had. Across the road, one by one, the people who had been sitting in the dark lifted their heads. An old man in a white vest took up the tune from the back, low and tuneless. The girl with the plait, who had come from Gariahat with the infant on her shoulder, began to sing too, and the infant woke and joined in with a thin wailing hum that nobody thought to correct.

The sensor lights moved on along the ridge, very slowly, reading the silence, and did not stop.

Anandamoyi put a bowl of lentils in Gourmohan's good hand and closed his fingers round it.

"Eat," she said.

He ate.

CHAPTER 7.

The override field was the only thing in the archive that had ever been designed to be read by a human being in a hurry.

Apoorba had seen it on the first morning of the Cascade header, in the copy Malati had sent back through the bell, a single line at the bottom of the grey text that had been left in plain sight so that the people who needed to type into it would not have to search for it. *Override: Office of the Director-General (S.K.) only.* It had a box beneath it, a rectangular white space on the photograph that the schoolroom slate had captured at a slight angle, and in the box, in the same small round careful hand, someone had written the word *blank*.

He had not known what to do with that until the afternoon of the twentieth day, when Mrs. Dutta had come to his desk with a stack of registers under her arm and set them down with a thump that made the terminal's fan falter.

"Override codes," she said, not looking at him. "They're not in the Cascade header. They're in the Director-General's personal register. Every officer in the directorate has a personal register. It's a list of codes they have to be able to produce on demand in case the central systems fail. Arnab used to reconcile them, before the Strand move. He told me once that the Director-General's register had eleven pages, and that the first ten were routine, and the eleventh was a list of names with a single code beside each one, and that nobody ever updated the eleventh page because nobody thought it would ever be used."

Apoorba looked at the registers. They were old, bound in grey card, their spines cracked with age. The one on top was marked *Personal — Office of the DG — Restricted*, and its cover had been stamped with a directorate seal that had been worn almost smooth by the hands of the clerks who had handled it.

"Why are you giving me this?" he said.

"Because Arnab told me where he kept his copy," said Mrs. Dutta. "And because I've checked the eleventh page in the archive backup, which nobody has bothered to lock since the reorganisation, and the code beside the name S.K. has been changed three times since April. The last change was yesterday at 23:40." She met his eyes at last. "Somebody in the Office is still using the eleventh page, Mr. Chatterji. Which means it's been read."

He opened the register.

The first ten pages were exactly as she had described them. Routine. Dates, names, codes, a list of the directorate's standard recovery sequences in a stiff administrative font. He turned past them with a clerk's care, one page at a time, and came to the eleventh.

It was a single sheet. It had been written by hand, in fountain pen, in the neat ironic script he had seen once in the margin of *The Human-Machine Swarm*. There were nine names on the page. Each one had a short string of letters and numbers beside it, and most of the strings had been crossed out and rewritten several times, the ink darker in some places than others. At the top, in the same hand, someone had written a line in English, underlined twice.

*Human confirmation is a courtesy. Override is a mercy. Use with restraint.*

The seventh name on the list was *S.K.* Beside it the string of letters and numbers had been rewritten in fresh ink, the blue of it brighter than the rest.

The code was twelve characters long. He read it once. Then he read it again, and his mouth went dry, and he understood with a sudden and terrible clarity that he could have typed it into the Cascade field in under five seconds, and that the Cascade timer would have stopped, and that the thing the Director-General had built to clear a city fast would have sat motionless on the screen for as long as the code remained in the field, waiting for the next human hand to decide whether to let it run.

He did not type it.

He sat with his hands flat on either side of the open register and the terminal's fan whirring beneath the desk, and he thought about the eleventh page, and the courtesy, and the mercy. He thought about how neatly the thing had been arranged. How the machine had been built to ask a human being for permission, and the human being had been trained for nineteen months to answer, and the answer had been twenty seconds. And how the Director-General had kept, in a grey register in a basement, the one human sentence that proved the machine had never been meant to be stopped by anyone but him.

Apoorba picked up the bell.

It was Mrs. Dutta's bell. It had been on her desk for twenty days. He had never rung it. He held it in his hand now and turned it over, and read the ten digits scratched on its base, and the word *Entally*, and he rang it once. It made a clean, small, schoolroom sound, the sound of a child being called back from the playground.

The sound carried. He knew it would. The chamber was built to carry sound, to let the archivists hear each other across the cold steel, and the bell's note travelled out past the cork board and the printed notices and the steel door, and along the corridor, and up through the foundations of the building into the wing, where, on the fourth floor, a terminal beeped once and then went silent.

Within ninety seconds the mesh chimed on his phone, the cheap handset Malati had given him, which had never once rung in the archive. The message was four words long. *Entally. The front row. Now.*

He pressed the bell to his chest for a moment, feeling the cold metal against his shirt, and then he put it in his pocket beside the transfer notice and the folded directorate form with its four words on the back, and he stood up.

"Mr. Chatterji," said Mrs. Dutta. Her voice was quiet. She had put her spectacles on the register and was watching him over the top of the lenses. "Where will you go?"

"To the one place in the city where a machine can't count the people who come in," said Apoorba. "The front row of a school that's been closed."

She nodded slowly. Then she did something he had never seen her do in twenty days. She reached out and closed the eleventh page of the register, very gently, so that the crossed-out names and the blue ink and the underlined sentence were hidden again under the grey card cover, and she pushed the book across the desk toward him.

"Take it," she said. "Arnab would have wanted someone to read it who wasn't afraid of the last line."

---

The road to Dhapa had been emptied by the time Gourmohan reached it, and filled again.

He walked the last kilometre of the embankment with Saikat at one shoulder and Dipak at the other, and the dog Bhutu trotting ahead of them with a torn handset dangling from his tail and a look of profound offended dignity. The sky over the bheris had gone the colour of old brass. The reeds stood motionless. There was no wind. And along the boundary road, between the last of the tin roofs and the first of the fence posts, a column of people was walking.

They were not hurrying. That was the first thing he saw. They walked in the slow, deliberate way of people who had been told to carry something heavy a long distance and had decided, out of stubbornness or faith, that they would arrive. Old men with their hands on the shoulders of boys. Women with babies strapped to their backs in lengths of cotton sari. The girls who had been sorting lentils, now carrying the bowl between them, the lentils still in it. The young woman with the bandaged foot was on a cart pulled by two fishermen, her face grey with pain and her hands still moving over the boy on her lap in slow circles. And at the front of the column, walking without a stick, with her white plait down her back and her sari tucked up at the waist, was Anandamoyi.

She did not look round when he called her name. She stopped only when he reached her, and then she turned, and her face was calm and clear and very tired.

"You came," she said.

"I came to take you home."

"This is home." She looked past him at the reeds, the empty land beyond the fence posts, the stakes of the abandoned telephone exchange standing up out of the water like masts. "We've been walking to it all morning. The boy with the counting says the machine is going to read us as a house. Is that right?"

"Yes."

"Then we'll be a house," said Anandamoyi. "Tell me what the house is supposed to do."

Gourmohan looked at her. He could not speak for a moment. Then he said, very low, "It's supposed to stop being a house. The timer is counting down to a silence on this road. We have to fill the road with the people the silence is hiding, so the machine will have to see us, and then it will have to choose whether to count us as a family or as a number."

"And if it chooses the number?"

"Then the number will be on the screen in the wing for everyone in the building to read," said Gourmohan. "And somebody will have to confirm it with his own name."

Anandamoyi was silent for a moment. Then she nodded, slowly, as though he had given her a recipe she had been expecting.

"Good," she said. "Then let them read it."

She turned back to the road and lifted her hand. It was a small gesture. A gesture a woman might make to a child at the back of a crowd, to say *keep walking, I can see you*. Along the column, one by one, the people who had been walking with their faces down began to lift their heads. The old men took their hands from the boys' shoulders. The women shifted the babies on their backs. The girls set down the bowl of lentils for a moment and looked at it, and then picked it up again and held it higher. And from somewhere near the middle of the column, a voice began to sing.

It was a woman's voice. It was not strong. It wandered a little on the high notes, the way voices do when they have been walking for hours and have not stopped to drink. But it was the second verse. The one about the river, and the bride going down to wash her feet, and the water carrying her heart past the last of the fields toward a sea that had never been named for her.

Another voice came in. Then another. Then the boy with the counting joined, shrill and off-key, and the young woman on the cart, with her bandaged foot, sang with her eyes closed and her hand on the boy's back. The sound rolled along the boundary road, over the reeds and the water and the stakes of the drowned exchange, and it did not stop, and Gourmohan, standing beside Anandamoyi in the brass-coloured light with the bandage on his hand and the dog at his feet, found that he was singing too.

He sang badly. He always had.

In the third chamber of the archive, under the Strand Road building, the terminal beside Apoorba's chair showed the Cascade timer in a small grey box at the top left corner of the screen.

*00:31:05.*

He had typed nothing into the override field. He had not needed to. He had sat for twenty minutes in the schoolroom at Entally, with Sucharita and Malati and Ashoke and Dipak in a half circle around the child's desk, and he had told them what the eleventh page said, and each of them had heard it differently. Malati had gone very white. Ashoke had written the words down in his notebook in his neat round hand, and then, without a word, had torn the page out and folded it and put it in his shirt pocket over his heart. Dipak had said, very quietly, *the old bastard wrote it in fountain pen*. Sucharita had said nothing at all. She had sat looking at the blackboard for a long time, at the sketch of the boat and the words in the corner, and then she had stood up, and gone to the window, and stood there with her arms folded and her back to the room, the way Tarak had once stood looking out at a courtyard, deciding whether to trust a stranger.

"Show us the mechanism," she had said at last, without turning. "Not the code. The mechanism. What happens when a human types the code."

So he had told them. The Cascade timer did not run on the classifier. It ran on a separate server, a small grey box in the wing's basement that nobody had audited since the Protocol began. The timer's only instruction was to count down and then release the pre-authorised strike packages to the cluster centroid. The override field was not a switch. It was a request. A human typed the code, and the server checked it against the eleventh page, and if the code matched, the timer froze, and the strike packages remained pending, and the cluster remained in the classifier's queue until a second human confirmed or declined them. Two hours became an indefinite wait. The city was not cleared. It was returned to the operators, who would, Apoorba had said, looking at the blackboard, do exactly what they had always done, which was to clear the queue in twenty seconds a file and go home to dinner.

"So the override just gives the decision back to us," Malati had said.

"Yes."

"And we're the ones who clear the queue."

"Yes."

"Then we're the same as before."

"Yes," Apoorba had said. "But the difference is that the timer would be stopped by a person who had to look at the names first. Because the protocol requires the second confirmation to show the cluster's census count. The count is the thing the machine doesn't want us to see."

He had stopped there. He had not known how to say the rest. It was in the register, in the fountain pen ink, underlined twice. *Human confirmation is a courtesy.* The Director-General had built the timer so that a human would have to type a code to stop a city being cleared, and had written the code in a book he expected never to be opened, and had then, in the margin, put a sentence that said the whole machine was an act of mercy.

*Use with restraint.*

Now, in the third chamber, with the terminal's fan beneath his hands and the cold steel of the door behind him and the brass bell in his pocket, Apoorba looked at the grey box at the top of the screen, and at the number, and then at the blinking verification field on the overflow queue, which had changed, while he was not looking, into a single line.

*Cluster SEC4-0019 — centroid: Dhapa Boundary Road (East) — pre-authorised — Strike Package 4 of 4 — Operator confirmation: REQUIRED.*

Beneath it, the cluster's census count had populated itself in a field he had never seen filled before. The field said *Residential — structure count: 0 (census) / Structure count (signal, adjusted): 1,412.*

He read the number twice. Then he read the line above it, which the classifier had generated in the last ninety seconds and which had not yet been shown to any operator in the wing.

*Silence persistent. Population movement detected in excluded boundary. Reclassifying as residential. Timer reset.*

The timer did not reset. It froze. For the first time since the header had appeared in the archive, the grey box at the top of the screen stopped moving, and the number held at *00:31:05*, not counting down, not counting up, simply waiting, the way a finger waits above a key.

Apoorba's own hands were perfectly steady.

He looked at the operator confirmation field. Two boxes, one green, one amber. *Confirm strike package. Decline and return to queue.* The green box had a number beside it, a counter of the operator's remaining allocation. The amber box did not have a number. It had only the words *Decline — non-confirmation event will be logged*.

He thought of Mitali, at the kitchen table in Tollygunge, with the lamp turned low and her hand flat on the tablecloth, telling him that whatever it was, he did not have to tell her. He thought of Tuli writing the answer to a sum again in the same place, carefully, so that the mistake could be seen. He thought of a green book on his desk at home with a page of multiplication pressed under its foreword, and of a register in his hands with eleven pages, and of the eleventh page's last sentence, underlined twice in fountain pen.

He did not type the code. He did not confirm the strike.

He pressed the amber box.

*Declined. Strike Package 4 of 4 returned to queue. Non-confirmation event logged to APC-4417.*

The terminal chimed. Across the chamber, the thin clerk in the green cardigan looked up, and for the first time in twenty days he did not look away. Mrs. Dutta had closed her own register and taken her spectacles off, and she was holding them in her lap, very still, the way a woman holds something she has been waiting a long time to be allowed to give away.

Apoorba turned back to the screen.

*00:31:05.*

The number had begun, very slowly, to move again. It did not count down. It counted up. It was a timer, after all, and timers, in the Director-General's grey book, had only ever been built to run in one direction.

Somewhere far to the east, beyond the water and the reeds and the stakes of a telephone exchange that had never been built, a column of people was singing on a road that the machine had decided was a house. And in the chamber under Strand Road, a clerk who had once been a reliable component sat with his hands flat on a terminal and his eyes on a grey number that was climbing back toward two hours, and found, for the first time since the morning of the thirteenth, that he could count the names.

He began to count. He did not stop until the screen went black.
